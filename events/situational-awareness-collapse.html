The Situational Awareness fund collapse

July 2026 · A textbook leverage blow-up

← All Big Events

The person who wrote the most famous essay arguing that AI would change everything built a hedge fund on exactly that idea — and then got wiped out when AI stocks dipped. His big-picture idea might still turn out right. Borrowed money just doesn’t give you time to be early.

The whole thing in plain English

A very smart 24-year-old borrowed a huge amount of money to bet that AI-related stocks (chips, data centers, electricity) would keep going up. For a while he was a genius: he turned $225 million into what was reported as around $45 billion.

Then those stocks dropped for a few weeks. Because so much of the money was borrowed, a medium-sized dip in the stocks turned into a giant loss for his fund. The banks that lent him the money got scared and forced him to sell everything, fast, at bad prices. In about one month he lost roughly two-thirds of the whole fund. That’s the entire story — the rest of this page explains the “why,” step by step.

The words you’ll need (in plain English)
Hedge fund
A private investment pool where rich people and institutions hand their money to a manager who tries to grow it with more aggressive tactics than a normal fund is allowed to use.
AUM (assets under management)
The total pile of money a fund is looking after. Bigger AUM = a bigger, more important fund.
Leverage
Investing with borrowed money so your bet is bigger than your own cash. It multiplies your winnings — and your losses — by the same amount.
Short selling
A way to bet that a price will go DOWN instead of up. If you short a stock and it falls, you make money.
Hedge
A side bet meant to protect you if your main bet goes wrong — basically insurance for your portfolio.
Margin call
When the bank that lent you money says: “your bet is losing — put in more cash right now, or we sell your stuff to protect our loan.” You don’t get to say no.
Prime broker
The big bank that lends a hedge fund money and holds its investments. Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan are examples.
Liquidity
How quickly you can turn something into cash. A stock is liquid (sell in seconds); a share of a private company is not (can take months).

1. Who is Leopold Aschenbrenner?

He’s a German-born prodigy, born around 2001. He finished Columbia University at the top of his class at just 19, studying economics and math. In 2023 he went to work at OpenAI (the company behind ChatGPT) on a team whose job was to keep future super-smart AI safe and under control.

In April 2024 OpenAI fired him. The company said he leaked information; he says the real reason was that he warned — in writing — that OpenAI wasn’t protected against foreign spies. Two months later, in June 2024, he published a long essay called “Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead”, predicting that AI would become as smart as humans around 2027 and reshape the world. It became one of the most talked-about documents in tech. Then he did something bold: he bet real money on his own prediction.

2. What was his bet? (The “shovels” idea)

In late 2024 he opened a hedge fund — a pool of money he invests for wealthy backers — called Situational Awareness LP. It started with about $225 million. His strategy was clever.

Picture this: In the 1849 Gold Rush, most people digging for gold went broke. But the folks who sold the shovels, picks and jeans got rich no matter who struck gold. Leopold’s bet was the modern version: don’t try to guess which AI company wins — instead, own the “shovels” that every AI company has to buy: the computer chips, the memory, the data centers, the electricity.

So he bought big stakes in companies like Oracle, AMD, memory-chip makers and power companies. It was a smart idea, and for 18 months it worked better than almost anyone in history: through June 2026 the fund was reportedly up +439%, and its money had grown from $225 million to a reported peak near $45 billion. The problem was never the idea. The problem was how he made the bet.

3. The secret ingredient — and the poison — was leverage

Leverage means investing with borrowed money so your bet is bigger than your own cash. Leopold used a lot of it — reportedly up to 4×. Here’s why that matters, with small numbers:

Picture this: You have $100. You’re so sure a stock will go up that you borrow $300 from a bank and buy $400 of it. If it rises 25%, you now have $500 — pay back the $300 loan and you’ve got $200. You doubled your money on a 25% move! But if it falls 25%, your $400 becomes $300 — you pay back the loan and you’re left with $0. The stock only dropped a quarter, but all your money is gone. That is leverage: it multiplies wins and losses by the same amount.

Now scale that up to billions. In early July 2026 the AI stocks he owned fell — Oracle and AMD each dropped about 20%, and smaller names fell more. A ~17% drop in his basket of stocks doesn’t sound fatal… until you remember it was ~4× leveraged:

4. The “doom loop” that finished it off

When you invest with borrowed money, the bank that lent it to you is watching nervously, because it’s their money at risk too. When your investments start losing, they make a margin call.

Picture this: Remember the $300 you borrowed? As your $400 of stock slides toward $300, the bank calls you: “Add more cash right now, or we sell your stock today to get our $300 back.” You can’t say no, and you don’t get to pick the price. That phone call is a margin call.

Now imagine everyone in the same AI trade gets that call on the same day. They all sell at once. All that selling pushes prices down even more — which triggers more margin calls — which forces more selling. It feeds on itself. Traders call this a doom loop, and it’s why the end of the chart is a cliff instead of a gentle slope. Leopold’s prime brokers (Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi) all wanted their loans made safe at the same time.

5. The insurance that didn’t work

Leopold wasn’t reckless on purpose — he thought he was protected. Alongside buying AI stocks, he also shorted (bet against) a bunch of software stocks. The plan: if the market fell, his short bets would go up and cushion the blow. That protective side bet is called a hedge.

But the hedge failed. The software stocks he bet against didn’t fall enough to help, so both sides of his book lost money at the same time — exactly when he needed one side to win. A hedge only helps if it actually moves the opposite way from your main bet. His didn’t.

6. The fire sale — how it ended

Around July 29–30, 2026, it was over. Unable to meet the margin calls, the fund sold its entire pile of stocks — about $16 billion worth — in roughly 24 hours. When you have to sell that much that fast, you take whatever price you can get. Most of it went to a single buyer big enough to swallow it: Ken Griffin’s Citadel, one of the largest funds in the world.

When the smoke cleared, the fund was down about 67% for the month and only around $10 billion was left.

And here’s the cruel twist: most of that remaining $10 billion wasn’t cash — it was a stake the fund owned in Anthropic, a private AI company. You can sell a normal stock in seconds, but you can’t quickly sell a piece of a private company. It’s like owning part of a house when you need cash tonight: it’s worth a lot, but you can’t spend it right now. That’s called being illiquid — and it meant the one valuable thing left couldn’t be used to stop the bleeding.

  • 2023

    Aschenbrenner joins OpenAI’s “Superalignment” team — the group trying to keep future super-smart AI under human control.

  • April 2024

    OpenAI fires him, saying he leaked information. He says the real reason was a memo he wrote warning that OpenAI wasn’t safe from foreign spying. The team is shut down soon after.

  • June 2024

    He publishes “Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead,” a ~165-page essay predicting that human-level AI arrives around 2027. It goes viral.

  • Late 2024

    He starts his own hedge fund, Situational Awareness LP, with about $225 million from famous tech backers (the Collison brothers, Daniel Gross, Nat Friedman).

  • Through mid-2026

    Betting big — with lots of borrowed money — on the AI boom, the fund reportedly grows +439% and swells toward a reported ~$45 billion. One of the great runs ever.

  • Early–mid July 2026

    AI and chip stocks start falling. Oracle and AMD each drop about 20%. His protective “short” bets don’t save him.

  • July 24, 2026

    Aschenbrenner tells his investors the fund “failed to escape” the sell-off.

  • ~July 29–30, 2026

    The banks demand their money back (margin calls). Forced to sell, the fund dumps its ENTIRE ~$16 billion stock portfolio in about a day — mostly to Ken Griffin’s Citadel. It ends the month down ~67%, with only ~$10 billion left, most of it stuck in a private Anthropic stake.

Why it matters — the lessons

  • Being right isn’t enough — you have to survive to see it. His AI idea could be 100% correct over ten years and still wipe him out over ten days, because borrowed money forced him to sell at the bottom. In plain terms: the market can stay scary longer than a borrowed bet can stay alive.
  • Leverage cuts both ways. The same 4× that made the +439% also made the −67%. Any gain you get from borrowing is a loss you can suffer just as fast.
  • Putting all your eggs in one basket is dangerous — especially when your “backup plan” is secretly tied to the same basket. Everything fell together right when he needed something to hold up.
  • When the bank calls, you’re not in charge anymore. Margin calls decide when and at what price you sell — not you.
  • Cash you can’t reach isn’t really cash. A great private investment is useless if you can’t sell it in the exact moment you need money.

This has happened before to very smart people. Long-Term Capital Management (1998) was run by Nobel Prize winners and blew up the same way. Archegos (2021) lost $20 billion in days on borrowed, concentrated bets. Different decade, same trap: brilliance + borrowed money + one big bet.
