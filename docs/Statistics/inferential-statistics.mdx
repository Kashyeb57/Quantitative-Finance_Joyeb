# Inferential Statistics & Hypothesis Testing

Inference is the art of saying something about a whole **population** from a limited **sample**. You never get to see all future returns, every customer, or the "true" mean — you get a handful of noisy observations and have to reason back to the thing that generated them, honestly quantifying how sure you're allowed to be.

## From sample to population

Take a sample of size $n$, compute its mean $\bar x$. Do it again with a fresh sample and you'll get a slightly different $\bar x$. The distribution of those means across hypothetical repeated samples is the **sampling distribution**, and the **Central Limit Theorem** says something almost magical: for large $n$, that distribution is approximately normal *regardless of the shape of the underlying data*, centered on the true mean $\mu$ with a spread — the **standard error** — of

$$
\operatorname{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.
$$

The $\sqrt{n}$ is the tax on precision: to halve your uncertainty you need **four times** the data. It's why a year of daily returns still leaves a stock's mean return badly pinned down.

## Confidence intervals

A 95% **confidence interval** is $\bar x \pm 1.96\,\operatorname{SE}$ (using the normal approximation). The honest interpretation is subtle: across many repeated samples, 95% of such intervals would contain the true $\mu$. It's a statement about the *procedure*, not a 95% probability about this one interval — that stronger claim is what the Bayesian **credible interval** gives you (see [Bayesian Inference](/docs/Statistics/bayesian-inference)).

## Hypothesis testing: the classic recipe

1. State a **null hypothesis** $H_0$ (usually "no effect," e.g. two groups have equal means) and an **alternative** $H_1$.
2. Compute a **test statistic** measuring how far the data sits from $H_0$, in standard-error units.
3. Get a **p-value**: the probability of a result at least this extreme *if $H_0$ were true*.
4. If $p$ is below a threshold $\alpha$ (often 0.05), reject $H_0$.

### Two groups: the t-test

Does using your phone slow your reaction time? Two samples, one test:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

no_phone = rng.normal(520, 60, 40)   # reaction times (ms)
phone    = rng.normal(560, 60, 40)   # true effect: +40 ms slower

t, p = stats.ttest_ind(phone, no_phone)
print(f"mean difference = {phone.mean() - no_phone.mean():.1f} ms")
print(f"t = {t:.2f},  p = {p:.4f}")
```

The $t$-statistic is (difference in means) ÷ (standard error of that difference). A big $|t|$ and a small $p$ say the gap is hard to explain as luck.

## Comparing many groups: ANOVA

With three or more groups, running lots of pairwise t-tests inflates your error rate. **ANOVA** tests them all at once by comparing the variance *between* groups to the variance *within* them — a big **F-statistic** means the groups differ by more than noise:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

group_a = rng.normal(30.0, 2.0, 30)   # lap times (s), training method A
group_b = rng.normal(31.0, 2.0, 30)   # method B
group_c = rng.normal(28.0, 2.0, 30)   # method C

F, p = stats.f_oneway(group_a, group_b, group_c)
print(f"F = {F:.2f},  p = {p:.4f}")
```

An ANOVA with a group predictor is, once again, just a [linear model / GLM](/docs/Statistics/regression-analysis) with dummy variables — same engine.

## Two ways to be wrong

| | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
|---|---|---|
| **Reject $H_0$** | Type I error (false positive), rate $\alpha$ | ✅ correct |
| **Fail to reject** | ✅ correct | Type II error (false negative), rate $\beta$ |

**Power** is $1-\beta$: the chance of detecting a real effect. It rises with sample size and effect size. Underpowered studies both miss real effects *and* — because only large flukes clear the bar — exaggerate the ones they do report.

## The trouble with p-values

- **"Not significant" ≠ "no effect."** It may just mean too little data.
- **Statistical ≠ practical.** With enough data, a trivially small effect becomes "significant." A tradable edge has to clear costs, not just $p<0.05$.
- **Multiple testing.** Test 20 strategies at $\alpha=0.05$ and you expect one "winner" by pure chance. Backtests are a p-hacking minefield.

## A better question: the ROPE

Instead of *"is the effect exactly zero?"* (almost never true in markets), ask *"is it big enough to care about?"* Define a **Region of Practical Equivalence (ROPE)** — a band around zero you'd treat as negligible, say $[-0.1, +0.1]$ — and, using the Bayesian posterior, measure how much of the effect's probability falls inside it:

$$
P(\theta \in \text{ROPE} \mid \text{data}) \;\begin{cases} > 0.99 & \text{practically zero} \\ < 0.01 & \text{a real effect} \\ \text{else} & \text{inconclusive} \end{cases}
$$

That middle "inconclusive" verdict — impossible to state cleanly with a p-value — is often the most honest thing you can say. It's the framework I used to judge a peer stock's effect in the [regression worked example](/docs/Statistics/regression-analysis).
