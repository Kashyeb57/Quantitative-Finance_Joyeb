# The 5-Number Summary

Five [percentiles](./percentiles-and-quartiles) capture the entire skeleton of a distribution — its center, its spread, and its extremes — in one compact summary. It's the basis of the **box plot** and of a beautifully simple, distribution-free way to flag **outliers**.

## The five numbers

$$
\text{minimum}, \quad Q_1, \quad \text{median}, \quad Q_3, \quad \text{maximum}
$$

- **Minimum** — the smallest value
- **Q1** — 25th percentile (bottom of the middle 50%)
- **Median** — 50th percentile (the center)
- **Q3** — 75th percentile (top of the middle 50%)
- **Maximum** — the largest value

For the sorted set $\left[1,2,2,2,3,3,4,5,5,5,6,6,6,6,7,8,8,9\right]$: minimum $=1$, $Q_1=3$, median $=5$, $Q_3=7$, maximum $=9$.

## The box plot

A **box plot** draws exactly these five numbers: a box spanning $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ (the middle 50% of the data), a line inside it at the median, and "whiskers" reaching out to the smallest and largest *non-outlier* values. It turns the five-number summary into a picture you can scan at a glance — and compare across many series side by side.

## The IQR and the outlier fences

The width of the box is the **interquartile range** — the spread of the middle half of the data, and a robust alternative to standard deviation:

$$
\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1
$$

The IQR also gives a distribution-free rule for flagging **outliers**. Anything beyond the *fences* is suspect:

$$
\text{lower fence} = Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}, \qquad \text{upper fence} = Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}
$$

Worked through with $Q_1=3$, $Q_3=7$: $\text{IQR}=4$, lower fence $=3-1.5(4)=-3$, upper fence $=7+1.5(4)=13$. So any value below $-3$ or above $13$ is an outlier. Append a **27** to that dataset and it lands far past the upper fence — flagged.

```python
import numpy as np

data = np.array([1,2,2,2,3,3,4,5,5,5,6,6,6,6,7,8,8,9, 27.0])  # 27 is a suspect
q1, med, q3 = np.percentile(data, [25, 50, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
lo, hi = q1 - 1.5*iqr, q3 + 1.5*iqr
outliers = data[(data < lo) | (data > hi)]

print(f"min={data.min():.0f}  Q1={q1:.1f}  median={med:.1f}  Q3={q3:.1f}  max={data.max():.0f}")
print(f"IQR={iqr:.1f}   fences=[{lo:.1f}, {hi:.1f}]   outliers={outliers}")
```

(One footnote: NumPy *interpolates* quartiles, so its $Q_1/Q_3$ can differ slightly from the hand-counted position method — both are accepted conventions, and the outlier verdict is the same.)

## Why a quant cares

The IQR fence is a cheap, **assumption-free** filter — it doesn't care whether your data is normal, which matters because [returns aren't](./histograms). Quants use it to flag **bad ticks** and fat-finger prints in raw price feeds, and to spotlight genuinely extreme return days for a closer look. Because it's built on quartiles rather than the mean and standard deviation, a single wild value can't corrupt the very fence that's supposed to catch it — the same robustness logic behind the median and [robust statistics](/docs/Statistics/robust-statistics).
