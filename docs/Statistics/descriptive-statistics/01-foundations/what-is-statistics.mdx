# What Is Statistics?

**Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, summarizing, analyzing, and interpreting data to make decisions under uncertainty.** That last phrase is the whole point: if the answer were certain you wouldn't need statistics — you'd just look it up. Statistics is the toolkit for reasoning when the data is noisy, incomplete, and only a *sample* of what you really care about.

Strip it to a sentence: **statistics turns raw numbers into a defensible conclusion.**

## A concrete example

Suppose College A has 1,000 students and you want to know the typical student's height.

- You could measure **all 1,000** and compute the average — say 165 cm. That single number *organizes and summarizes* a thousand measurements into one fact. That's statistics doing its most basic job.
- Or you might only measure **50** students and use them to *estimate* the average height of all 1,000 — or of next year's intake, whom you can't measure at all. Now you're reaching beyond the data in hand, and you need to be honest about how uncertain that reach is.

Those two moves — *summarize what you have* versus *generalize to what you don't* — are the [two branches of statistics](./types-of-statistics), and telling them apart is the first real skill.

## Why a quant cares

For a quant, statistics isn't one tool among many — it's the ground the entire job stands on. Every claim you make about a market is a statistical claim:

- *"This signal has an edge"* — is the average return reliably above zero, or is it luck?
- *"This loss is normal"* — how far from typical is today's move?
- *"These two assets diversify each other"* — how strongly do their returns move together?
- *"This backtest will hold up live"* — does the past sample generalize to the future?

Answering any of these well is applied statistics. And markets punish sloppy statistics faster than almost any other field — a flawed inference doesn't just get a bad grade, it loses money. That's why this section starts at the foundations and keeps returning to one theme: **knowing when the data actually supports your conclusion, and when it's fooling you.**
