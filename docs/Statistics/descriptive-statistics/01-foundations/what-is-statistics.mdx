# What Is Statistics?

**Statistics is the field that deals with the collection, organization, analysis, interpretation, and presentation of data.** Every one of those steps points at a single destination: **decision making.** You gather data, make sense of it, and act — that's the whole arc, and if a step doesn't ultimately help someone *decide* something, it isn't doing its job.

Strip it to a sentence: **statistics turns raw numbers into a defensible decision.**

## From data to a decision

The field is built around one workflow. Take a real scrap of data — say the ages of customers on an online-shopping site:

$$
\text{Age} = \left[24, 27, 14, 13, 28, 29, 31, 32\right]
$$

1. **Summarize it.** Compute the **mean** age and the **median** age — one or two numbers that stand in for the whole list (these are [measures of central tendency](/docs/Statistics/descriptive-statistics/summary-measures/central-tendency)).
2. **Describe its distribution.** Beyond a single number: how are the ages *spread* — bunched in the late 20s, or all over the place?
3. **Visualize it** with charts and graphs — a **histogram**, and its smooth relatives the **PDF** (probability density function) and **CDF** (cumulative distribution function).
4. **Understand the data and make a decision** — which products to show, which age group to target.
5. For a business, a better decision means one thing: **grow the business.**

That chain — *data → summarize & visualize → understand → decide* — is statistics in one breath. (The histogram shows up later in [this module](/docs/Statistics/descriptive-statistics/shape-and-position/histograms); the PDF and CDF, which describe a distribution's shape and its running total, live in the [Probability section](/docs/Probability/distributions).)

## Two ways to use the data

There's a fork in that workflow worth naming now, because everything downstream depends on it:

- **Summarize what you have** — describe the data in hand, and stop there.
- **Generalize to what you don't** — use the sample to reach a conclusion about a larger group you couldn't measure.

Those are the [two branches of statistics](./types-of-statistics), and telling them apart is the first real skill.

## Where statistics is used

The lecture title says "*and its application*" for a reason — this is one of the most widely used tools there is:

- **Almost everyone uses it**, often without noticing — even everyday budgeting and planning is informal statistics.
- **A bank** aggregates transaction **data** from the ATMs at each of its branches (Location A, Location B, …) and turns it into a **statistical decision** — where to place the next machine, how to staff, how to flag fraud.
- **A clinical trial** — does a new vaccine actually work? — is settled by a statistical test on experimental data.
- **As a profession:** machine-learning engineers, data analysts, business analysts, and **risk analysts** all do statistics for a living. It reaches into essentially every field.

## Why a quant cares

For a quant, statistics isn't one tool among many — it's the ground the entire job stands on. Every claim you make about a market is a statistical claim:

- *"This signal has an edge"* — is the average return reliably above zero, or is it luck?
- *"This loss is normal"* — how far from typical is today's move?
- *"These two assets diversify each other"* — how strongly do their returns move together?
- *"This backtest will hold up live"* — does the past sample generalize to the future?

Answering any of these well is applied statistics — the same *data → understand → decide* loop as the shopping site and the bank, just with returns instead of ages and money on the line. And markets punish sloppy statistics faster than almost any other field: a flawed inference doesn't get a bad grade, it loses money. That's why this section starts at the foundations and keeps returning to one theme: **knowing when the data actually supports your conclusion, and when it's fooling you.**
