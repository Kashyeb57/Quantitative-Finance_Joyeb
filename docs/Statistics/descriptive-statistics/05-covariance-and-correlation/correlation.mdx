# Correlation

[Covariance](./covariance) gets the *direction* of a relationship right but leaves its *strength* uninterpretable — the magnitude is unbounded and changes with units. **Correlation fixes this by normalizing covariance into a fixed, comparable range.** It's the number quants actually use.

## Pearson correlation coefficient

Divide covariance by the two standard deviations, and the units cancel — squeezing the result into a clean $[-1, 1]$:

$$
\rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}
$$

Now the number is directly interpretable:

- **$\rho = +1$** — perfect positive **linear** relationship (the points lie on an upward line).
- **$\rho = -1$** — perfect negative linear relationship (a downward line).
- **$\rho = 0$** — no *linear* relationship.
- The closer to $\pm 1$, the tighter the linear co-movement; the closer to $0$, the weaker.

The crucial qualifier is **linear**. Pearson measures *straight-line* association specifically. A relationship can be perfectly predictable yet curved, and Pearson will report less than 1 — it only sees the straight-line part.

## Spearman rank correlation

**Spearman correlation is Pearson computed on the *ranks* of the data** instead of the raw values. Replace each value by its position in sorted order, then correlate those ranks:

$$
\rho_s = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}\big(R(X),\,R(Y)\big)}{\sigma_{R(X)}\,\sigma_{R(Y)}}
$$

Because it only cares about **order**, Spearman captures any **monotonic** relationship — one where $Y$ consistently rises (or consistently falls) as $X$ rises, even along a bent curve. If $Y$ always increases with $X$ but on a curve, **Pearson reads below 1 while Spearman reads exactly 1.** Two practical consequences: use Spearman when the link is monotonic-but-nonlinear, and lean on it when **outliers** would distort Pearson (ranks are robust — the largest value is just "rank $n$," however extreme).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

x = rng.normal(0, 1, 300)
y = 0.8*x + rng.normal(0, 0.6, 300)             # linearly related to x

print(f"Pearson r (x, y):        {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0,1]: .3f}   (linear strength)")

# a monotonic but strongly NON-linear link
z = np.exp(3*x)
print(f"Pearson  (x, e^(3x)):    {stats.pearsonr(x, z)[0]: .3f}   <- fooled by the curve")
print(f"Spearman (x, e^(3x)):    {stats.spearmanr(x, z)[0]: .3f}   <- =1: perfectly monotonic")
```

## Feature selection: a worked intuition

Say you're predicting a **house price** and you have candidate features. Correlation with price tells you which carry signal:

- **Size**, **number of rooms**, **location quality** → positively correlated with price. Useful features.
- **"Is it haunted?"** → negatively correlated with price. Also useful (in the other direction).
- **Number of people currently staying in the house** → roughly **zero** correlation with price. Drop it; it adds noise, not signal.

This is the essence of **feature selection**: keep the features that correlate with the target, drop the ones that don't — and watch for two features highly correlated *with each other*, since they carry **redundant** information and you can often keep just one. (More in [Regression & Classification](/docs/Machine_Learning/regression-and-classification).)

## Why a quant cares

Correlation is woven through the whole job:

- **Diversification and portfolio risk are built on it.** Two assets with high positive correlation barely diversify each other; low or negative correlation is what smooths a portfolio's equity curve. A **correlation matrix** is the raw input to portfolio construction and risk models.
- **Hedging** means deliberately holding negatively (or highly positively) correlated positions to cancel a risk.
- **Feature selection**, as above, keeps models lean and less prone to overfitting.

## The warning that never goes away

**Correlation is not causation.** Two series can move together because one drives the other, because a hidden third factor drives both, or by pure chance over a short window. A high $\rho$ is a *clue*, never a proof. Deciding which variable actually *drives* which needs more than a coefficient — it needs the causal reasoning in [Causal Inference & DAGs](/docs/Statistics/causal-inference).
