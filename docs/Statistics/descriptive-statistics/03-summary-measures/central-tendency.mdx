# Measures of Central Tendency

A measure of central tendency answers the first question you ask of any dataset: **where is it centered?** There are three answers, and the gaps between them are as informative as the numbers themselves.

## Mean

The **arithmetic mean** is the average — sum the values, divide by how many. It uses *every* data point, which is both its strength and its weakness.

$$
\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i \;\;\text{(population)} \qquad
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \;\;\text{(sample)}
$$

For ages $\left[1, 3, 4, 5\right]$: $\bar{x} = \frac{1+3+4+5}{4} = \frac{13}{4} = 3.25$.

## Median

The **median** is the middle value once the data is **sorted**. It depends only on rank, so it ignores *how* extreme the extremes are.

- **Odd count** → the single middle value. Sort $\left[4, 3, 1, 5, 100\right]$ to $\left[1, 3, 4, 5, 100\right]$; the median is the 3rd value, **4**.
- **Even count** → the average of the two middle values. Sort $\left[4, 3, 1, 5, 100, 200\right]$ to $\left[1, 3, 4, 5, 100, 200\right]$; the middle two are 4 and 5, so the median is $\frac{4+5}{2} = 4.5$.

## Mode

The **mode** is the most frequently occurring value. It's the only measure of center that works on [categorical data](/docs/Statistics/descriptive-statistics/variables/variables) (the most common sector, the most common rating) and it's useful for discrete counts. A dataset can have one mode, several, or none.

## The key difference: robustness to outliers

This is the practical lesson. Watch what one extreme value does.

Ages $\left[1, 3, 4, 5\right]$: mean $= 3.25$, median $= 3.5$ — they agree. Now add a 100-year-old:

$$
\text{Ages} = \left[1, 3, 4, 5, 100\right] \Rightarrow \bar{x} = \frac{113}{5} = 22.6, \quad \text{median} = 4
$$

One outlier dragged the **mean** from ~3 to 22.6 — a value that describes *none* of the data. The **median** barely moved, from 3.5 to 4. The mean is pulled by extremes; the median shrugs them off. That property is called **robustness**.

```python
import numpy as np

# daily returns (%), with one crash day
rets = np.array([0.4, -0.2, 0.1, 0.3, -0.1, 0.2, -9.0])

print(f"mean:   {np.mean(rets):+.3f}%   <- dragged negative by the single crash")
print(f"median: {np.median(rets):+.3f}%   <- barely moves; robust to the outlier")
```

## Why a quant cares

Market returns are **skewed and fat-tailed**, so the mean and median disagree far more than in a textbook — and the disagreement is a signal:

- A month of 19 quiet days plus one $-9\%$ crash has a **negative mean** but a mildly **positive median**. Which you report changes the entire story of that month.
- The **gap** between mean and median is itself a rough read on skew: mean below median hints at a fat *left* tail (crash risk); mean above median hints at a fat *right* tail.
- Because a single print can hijack the mean, robust centers (the median, and its cousins in [robust statistics](/docs/Statistics/robust-statistics)) are often the safer summary of "typical" behavior.
