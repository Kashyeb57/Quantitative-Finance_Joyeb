# Confidence Interval & Margin of Error

So far every test in this module — [z-test](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/tests-for-a-mean/z-test), [t-test](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/tests-for-a-mean/t-test) — took a **sample mean** and asked whether it was too far from a *claimed* population mean to be luck. For a 95% two-tailed test we drew the familiar picture: the middle **95%** is where we **accept the null**, and the two extreme ends — **2.5%** each — are where we **reject** it.

But there's a question we never actually answered: **what values could lie *within* that confidence interval?** That flips the whole exercise from *testing* a claim to *estimating* the unknown mean — and it's where the confidence interval earns its name.

## The problem with a point estimate

Recall the [point estimate](/docs/Probability/distributions/sampling-and-estimation/estimates): the sample mean $\bar x$ is our single best guess for the population mean $\mu$.

$$
\bar x \;\longrightarrow\; \mu \qquad (\text{the point estimate stands in for the population mean})
$$

Here's the trouble. Suppose the true mean is $\mu = 3$ and one sample gives $\bar x = 2.5$. That's **less than 3.** Draw a *different* sample and $\bar x$ might come out **greater than 3.** Every sample lands somewhere slightly different, so a lone number is silent about how far off it might be. **That is the problem of using a point estimate.**

The fix is to stop reporting one number and instead **define a range** — "my estimate lies between **2 and 4**." That range, tied to a confidence level, is the **confidence interval**, and it honestly represents an estimate of an unknown population parameter.

## The formula

A confidence interval runs from a **lower** bound to a **higher** bound, and it's built by one simple recipe:

$$
\boxed{\;\textbf{Confidence Interval} = \textbf{Point Estimate} \;\pm\; \textbf{Margin of Error}\;}
$$

We use $\pm$ because the point estimate will be a little **less than**, a little **more than**, or roughly **equal to** the true mean — so we reach out on both sides. The point estimate is $\bar x$; the margin of error is where the test machinery comes back:

$$
\underbrace{\bar x}_{\text{point estimate}} \;\pm\; \underbrace{z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}}_{\text{margin of error (z-test)}}
$$

- **$z_{\alpha/2}$** is the critical value that carves off area $\alpha/2$ in each tail — read straight from the **z-table**, exactly as in the z-test.
- **$\sigma/\sqrt n$** is the [standard error](/docs/Probability/distributions/sampling-and-estimation/central-limit-theorem).

If instead you're doing a **t-test** (population $\sigma$ unknown), the shape is identical — you just swap the z-table for the **t-table** and use the sample $s$, remembering to compute the **degrees of freedom**:

$$
\bar x \;\pm\; t_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \qquad (\text{df} = n-1)
$$

## Worked example — the CAT exam

> On the verbal section of the **CAT exam**, the population standard deviation is **known to be 100** (σ is known ⇒ use a **z-test**). A sample of **30 test takers** has a **mean of 520**. **Construct a 95% confidence interval about the mean.**

**Set up the critical value.** With 95% confidence, $\alpha = 0.05$, so $\alpha/2 = 0.025$. From the z-table, the area to the left of the upper cutoff is $1 - 0.025 = 0.9750$, which maps to

$$
z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.025} = 1.96 \qquad (\text{and the lower cutoff is } -1.96).
$$

**Lower confidence interval:**

$$
\text{Lower C.I.} = 520 - (1.96)\frac{100}{\sqrt{25}} = 520 - 39.2 = \boxed{480.8}
$$

**Higher confidence interval:**

$$
\text{Higher C.I.} = 520 + (1.96)\frac{100}{\sqrt{25}} = 520 + 39.2 = \boxed{559.2}
$$

**Conclusion:** *I am **95% confident** that the mean CAT exam score lies between **480.8 and 559.2**.* If a claimed mean falls inside this interval we'd accept the null; if it falls outside, we'd reject it — the interval and the test are two views of the same thing.

:::note A slip in the source numbers
The problem says **30** test takers, but the worked arithmetic uses $\sqrt{25}$ (i.e. $n = 25$) to reach the quoted $[480.8, 559.2]$. I've kept the notes' published answer. For the record, with $n = 30$ the margin would be $1.96 \times 100/\sqrt{30} \approx 35.8$, giving $[484.2,\ 555.8]$ — same method, slightly tighter interval.
:::

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def conf_interval(xbar, sigma, n, conf=0.95, sigma_known=True):
    alpha = 1 - conf
    se = sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    if sigma_known:                      # z-test
        crit = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2)
    else:                                # t-test (sigma estimated by s)
        crit = stats.t.ppf(1 - alpha/2, df=n-1)
    moe = crit * se
    return xbar - moe, xbar + moe, crit, moe

lo, hi, crit, moe = conf_interval(xbar=520, sigma=100, n=25, conf=0.95)
print(f"critical z = {crit:.2f},  margin of error = ±{moe:.1f}")
print(f"95% CI = [{lo:.1f}, {hi:.1f}]")     # [480.8, 559.2]
print("I am 95% confident the mean CAT score is in this range.")
```

## Why a quant cares

The margin of error is the number risk reports quietly drop — and dropping it is how a point estimate masquerades as certainty:

- **Every reported statistic deserves a ± band.** A mean return, a volatility, a Sharpe ratio — reporting "0.9 Sharpe" hides that the 95% interval might be $[0.1,\ 1.7]$. The *width*, driven by $\sigma/\sqrt n$, is the honest measure of how much you actually know.
- **The $\sqrt n$ in the margin is the tax on precision.** To halve the margin of error you need **four times** the data — which is why a short track record produces a uselessly wide interval no matter how good the point estimate looks.
- **Confidence level is a dial with a cost.** Demanding 99% instead of 95% widens the interval (the critical value grows from 1.96 to 2.58); more certainty buys you a vaguer range. Choosing it is a judgment call about how badly a miss would hurt.

Report the point, but **size your decisions to the interval** — the band, not the center, is what tells you whether you can survive being wrong.
