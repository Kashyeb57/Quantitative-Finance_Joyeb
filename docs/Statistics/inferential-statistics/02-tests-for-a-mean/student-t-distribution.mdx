# The Student-t Distribution

The [z-test](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/tests-for-a-mean/z-test) has a hidden requirement. When we perform any analysis using a **z-score**, we require $\sigma$ — the **population** standard deviation — to be **already known**:

$$
z = \frac{\bar x - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
$$

But in almost every real problem, **we don't know $\sigma$.** We only have a sample. So:

> **How do we perform an analysis when we don't know the population standard deviation?**

The answer is **Student's t-distribution.**

## Estimate σ with the sample, and the z becomes a t

The fix is natural: replace the unknown population $\sigma$ with the **sample standard deviation $s$** computed from your data. That single swap changes the statistic — and the distribution it follows:

$$
\underbrace{z = \frac{\bar x - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}}_{\text{z-table, } \sigma \text{ known}} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{t = \frac{\bar x - \mu}{s/\sqrt n}}_{\text{t-table, } \sigma \text{ unknown}}
$$

where **$s$ = sample standard deviation.** You now look the value up in the **t-table** instead of the z-table — that lookup *is* the **[t-test](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/tests-for-a-mean/t-test).**

Why isn't it still a normal? Because $s$ is itself an *estimate*, wobbling from sample to sample. That extra uncertainty has to go somewhere, and it shows up as **fatter tails**: the t-distribution admits more extreme values than the normal, so its critical values sit farther out. The smaller the sample, the fatter the tails.

## Degrees of freedom

The t-distribution isn't one curve — it's a family indexed by **degrees of freedom (dof)**:

$$
\text{dof} = n - 1
$$

For a sample of 3 people, $\text{dof} = 3 - 1 = 2$. Where does the "$-1$" come from? Once you fix the sample mean, the values are no longer all free to vary. Picture **3 people** whose average is pinned: you can choose the first two freely, but the **third is then forced** to whatever value makes the mean come out right.

So $n-1$ counts the values that were genuinely free to vary after the mean ate up one degree of freedom. It's the same reason [sample variance divides by $n-1$](/docs/Statistics/descriptive-statistics/summary-measures/why-n-minus-1), not $n$.

## t versus normal — watch the tails converge

Below, switch the distribution to **t** and drag the **degrees of freedom**. At low dof the t (solid) is visibly shorter and fatter-tailed than the normal (dashed), and its critical value sits farther out. Push dof toward 30–40 and the two curves become nearly indistinguishable — **the t-distribution converges to the normal as the sample grows**, which is exactly why the z-test is fine once $n \ge 30$.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Two-tailed 95% critical value: t (df = n−1) vs the normal's 1.96.
print(f"{'n':>4} {'dof':>4} {'t crit':>8} {'z crit':>8}")
for n in (2, 3, 5, 10, 30, 100):
    dof = n - 1
    t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, dof)
    print(f"{n:>4} {dof:>4} {t_crit:>8.3f} {1.96:>8.2f}")

# Small samples demand a MORE extreme statistic to reach significance:
# t crit shrinks from ~12.7 (n=2) toward 1.96 as n grows.
```

## Why a quant cares

Finance lives in the small-sample, unknown-σ world the t-distribution was built for:

- **You never know a strategy's true volatility** — you estimate it from returns as $s$. So a Sharpe-ratio significance test is a **t-test**, not a z-test, and with only a year or two of data the fatter t-tails mean you need a *bigger* observed Sharpe to call it real.
- **The t's fat tails are a feature, not a nuisance.** Underestimating how often extreme values occur is precisely the error that blows up risk models; the t builds that humility into the critical value.
- **"$n \ge 30$" is a rule of thumb, not a law.** With genuinely fat-tailed returns even 30 points can be too few, and the gap between t and z matters most exactly when data is scarce and stakes are high.

The t-distribution is the honest default: use it whenever you had to *estimate* the spread — which is almost always.
