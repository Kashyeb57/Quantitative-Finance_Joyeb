# Chi-Square for Goodness of Fit — a Worked Problem

Now we actually *run* the [chi-square test](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/categorical-tests/chi-square-test) from the last lesson — a full hypothesis test, step by step, using the **chi-square table**.

## The problem

> In the **2010 census** of a small city, the **weights** of individuals were distributed as:
>
> | Weight | &lt;50 kg | 50–75 | &gt;75 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Proportion** | 20% | 30% | 50% |
>
> In **2020**, the weights of **n = 500** individuals were sampled, with these results:
>
> | Weight | &lt;50 | 50–75 | &gt;75 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Count** | 140 | 160 | 200 |
>
> Using **α = 0.05**, would you conclude the population distribution of weights has **changed** in the last 10 years?

There are **three categories**, so chi-square is the right tool. The 2010 percentages are the **theory** — the **expected** distribution; the 2020 sample is the **observed** distribution.

## Build the expected counts

The 2010 proportions still hold under the null, but we need them as **counts out of the 2020 sample of 500** — so multiply each proportion by 500:

$$
E_{<50} = 0.2\times 500 = 100, \qquad E_{50\text{–}75} = 0.3\times 500 = 150, \qquad E_{>75} = 0.5\times 500 = 250
$$

| Weight | Observed (O) | Expected (E) |
|---|---|---|
| &lt;50 | 140 | 100 |
| 50–75 | 160 | 150 |
| &gt;75 | 200 | 250 |
| **Total** | **500** | **500** |

## The five steps

**① Hypotheses.**

$$
H_0:\ \text{the data meets the expectation} \qquad H_1:\ \text{the data does *not* meet the expectation}
$$

**② Significance level.** $\alpha = 0.05$, so $\text{C.I.} = 95\%$.

**③ Degrees of freedom.** For goodness of fit, df is the number of categories minus one:

$$
\text{df} = k - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2
$$

**④ Decision boundary.** Here's what's different from the [z](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/tests-for-a-mean/z-test)/[t](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/tests-for-a-mean/t-test) tests: those used a **symmetric** bell, but the **chi-square distribution is not symmetric** — it's right-skewed and can't go negative. So the test is **one-tailed**: the entire rejection region (area $\alpha$) sits in the **right tail**. We find one **critical value** from the χ² table using df and $\alpha$, and reject $H_0$ if the statistic lands beyond it.

**Reading the chi-square table.** The table's top row is the area *to the right* of the critical value. At **df = 2** and $\alpha = 0.05$:

| df | 0.10 | **0.05** | 0.025 | 0.01 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.706 | 3.841 | 5.024 | 6.635 |
| **2** | 4.605 | **5.991** | 7.378 | 9.210 |
| 3 | 6.251 | 7.815 | 9.348 | 11.345 |
| 4 | 7.779 | 9.488 | 11.143 | 13.277 |
| 5 | 9.236 | 11.070 | 12.833 | 15.086 |

So the **critical value is 5.991**. The rule:

$$
\text{if } \chi^2 > 5.991 \Rightarrow \textbf{reject } H_0, \qquad \text{else} \Rightarrow \textbf{fail to reject } H_0.
$$

**⑤ Calculate the chi-square test statistic.**

$$
\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}
$$

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(140-100)^2}{100} + \frac{(160-150)^2}{150} + \frac{(200-250)^2}{250} = \frac{1600}{100} + \frac{100}{150} + \frac{2500}{250}
$$

$$
\chi^2 = 16 + 0.66 + 10 = \boxed{26.66}
$$

## The verdict

$$
26.66 > 5.991 \ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{reject } H_0.
$$

**Conclusion:** the weights of the 2020 population are **different** from those expected under the 2010 distribution. Looking at *where* the counts diverge, the shift is toward the **lighter** end — the &lt;50 bin came in *above* expectation (140 vs 100) while the &gt;75 bin came in *below* (200 vs 250) — so the distribution genuinely changed shape over the decade.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

observed = np.array([140, 160, 200])          # 2020 sample, n = 500
props    = np.array([0.20, 0.30, 0.50])        # 2010 census proportions
expected = props * observed.sum()              # -> [100, 150, 250]

chi2, p = stats.chisquare(f_obs=observed, f_exp=expected)
df = len(observed) - 1
crit = stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, df)                # critical value at α = 0.05

print(f"expected counts : {expected}")
print(f"chi-square stat : {chi2:.2f}")         # 26.66
print(f"df = {df},  critical (α=0.05) = {crit:.3f}")   # 2, 5.991
print(f"p-value         : {p:.6f}")
print("decision        :", "REJECT H0 — distribution changed" if chi2 > crit
      else "fail to reject H0")
```

## Why a quant cares

The goodness-of-fit test is the formal answer to *"has the distribution changed?"* — a question that runs through risk and trading:

- **Regime / distribution drift.** Bucket returns (or volatility, or volume) into ranges and compare this year's observed counts against last year's — or against your model's assumed distribution. A big χ² is a statistical alarm that **the world your model was fit on no longer holds**.
- **Backtest sanity checks.** If a strategy assumes signals fall into certain outcome categories at certain rates, goodness of fit tests whether the *realized* frequencies match — catching a broken assumption before it costs money.
- **One category can dominate.** Here the &lt;50 bin alone contributed 16 of the 26.66. The per-category $(O-E)^2/E$ terms tell you *where* the misfit lives — which bucket drove the rejection — not just that a misfit exists.

Where a z/t test asks "did the *average* move?", chi-square asks "did the *whole shape* move?" — often the more important question when tails are what hurt you.
