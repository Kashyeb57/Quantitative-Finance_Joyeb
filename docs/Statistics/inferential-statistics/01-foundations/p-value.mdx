# What Is a P-value?

**The p-value is a number, calculated from a statistical test, that describes how likely you are to have found a particular set of observations *if the null hypothesis were true*. P-values are used in hypothesis testing to help decide whether to reject the null hypothesis.**

Read that definition twice, because almost everyone misreads it. The p-value is **not** the probability the null is true. It is the probability of seeing data *at least this extreme* **in a world where $H_0$ holds**. Small p-value ⇒ "what I observed would be a weird fluke if the null were true" ⇒ evidence against the null.

## The intuition: how often do you land here?

Picture pressing a space bar 100 times and marking where your finger lands. Most presses cluster in a likely region; a few stray to an unlikely one. If a spot is one where you'd land **around 20 times out of 100**, its "p-value" is about $0.2$ — perfectly ordinary. A spot you'd hit only rarely carries a *tiny* p-value — landing there is surprising, and surprise is exactly what makes you doubt the default assumption.

$$
p = P(\text{data this extreme, or more} \mid H_0 \text{ true})
$$

## The coin example

> **Is a coin fair or not?** Flip it **100 times.**

Under a fair coin we'd expect the probabilities to be balanced; an unfair coin tilts them:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H) &= 0.5, & P(T) &= 0.5 \quad(\text{fair})\\
P(H) &= 0.6, & P(T) &= 0.4\\
P(H) &= 0.7, & P(T) &= 0.3
\end{aligned}
$$

Run the mechanism from the [previous lesson](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/foundations/hypothesis-testing-mechanism):

1. **Null hypothesis $H_0$:** the coin **is fair**.
2. **Alternate hypothesis $H_1$:** the coin **is not fair**.
3. **Experiment:** flip it **100 times** and count heads.
4. **Significance value:** choose $\alpha = 0.05$, so the **confidence interval** is $\text{C.I.} = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95$.
5. **Conclusion:** compare $p$ against $\alpha$.

## Where α lives: the 0.025 tails

With $\alpha = 0.05$ split across a **two-tailed** test, the middle **95%** is the region where "the coin is fair" is believable, and **0.025 in each tail** is the **rejection region** where we'd conclude "the coin is not fair." A result of ~50 heads sits comfortably in the middle; a result far out in a tail — say 70 heads — is where the null dies.

## The decision rule

$$
\boxed{\;p < \alpha \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{Reject the null hypothesis}\;}
$$

$$
\text{else} \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{Fail to reject the null hypothesis}
$$

That's the entire payoff. If your observations would be too improbable under $H_0$ (p below your threshold), you reject $H_0$. Otherwise the evidence isn't strong enough and you retain it.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Observed: 70 heads in 100 flips. Is the coin fair?
# H0: p = 0.5 (fair)   H1: p != 0.5 (two-tailed)
heads, n, alpha = 70, 100, 0.05

# exact two-sided p-value under H0 (binomial test)
p_value = stats.binomtest(heads, n, 0.5, alternative='two-sided').pvalue
print(f"observed heads : {heads}/{n}")
print(f"p-value        : {p_value:.6f}")
print("decision       :",
      "REJECT H0 — coin is not fair" if p_value < alpha
      else "fail to reject H0 — no evidence it's unfair")

# 50 heads, by contrast, is the least surprising result possible:
print("\n50/100 p-value:", stats.binomtest(50, n, 0.5).pvalue)  # = 1.0
```

## Why a quant cares

The p-value is where good intentions go to die, so a quant has to hold two ideas at once:

- **It answers the right conditional the wrong way round.** $p$ is $P(\text{data}\mid H_0)$, never $P(H_0\mid \text{data})$. Confusing them — the "prosecutor's fallacy" — is how a 1-in-20 fluke gets sold as a 95%-sure edge.
- **A small p is necessary, not sufficient.** With enough data an economically meaningless effect clears $p<0.05$; a real edge still has to beat costs and slippage. **Statistical significance ≠ tradable significance.**
- **Every extra test spends your α.** Try 20 strategies at $\alpha=0.05$ and you *expect* one "winner" from noise alone. That's the [multiple-testing](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics) trap that makes backtests so treacherous — the topic the [big-picture page](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/foundations/big-picture) opens with.

Use the p-value as a *speed bump* — "could this plausibly be luck?" — not as a probability that you're right.
