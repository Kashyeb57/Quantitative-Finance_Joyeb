# Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

The [z](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/tests-for-a-mean/z-test)- and [t](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/tests-for-a-mean/t-test)-tests compare **one** mean, or at most **two** groups. But what if you have **three or four** groups and want to know whether *any* of them differ? That's the job of **ANOVA.**

## Definition

> **ANOVA is a statistical method used to compare the means of two or more groups.**

That's the one line to remember. With two group means you'd reach for a z- or t-test; the moment you have **three groups, four groups** — several groups you want to analyze and compare at once — you use **ANOVA** instead.

:::note Why not just run many t-tests?
Comparing every pair with separate t-tests inflates the chance of a false positive (each test spends its own $\alpha$). ANOVA tests them **all together** with a single test — the reason it exists is spelled out on the [Big Picture page](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/foundations/big-picture). The *how* — an F-statistic from partitioned variance — comes later in this module.
:::

## The two components: factors and levels

Every ANOVA problem is organized around **two** pieces of vocabulary. Get these and the data layout falls into place.

**① Factor (the variable).** The categorical variable whose effect you're studying. (A factor can be *independent* or *dependent* — that distinction comes in the next lesson, on assumptions and types.)

**② Levels.** The specific groups or settings *within* a factor.

An example makes it concrete. Take **medicine** as the **factor** — and suppose it's given at different **dosages: 5 mg, 10 mg, 15 mg.** Those dosages are the **levels.**

A second, everyday example: **mode of payment** is the **factor**, and the ways people pay — **GPay, PhonePe, IMPS, NEFT** (and demand draft, and so on) — are its **levels.** Whatever the domain, the pattern is the same: **a factor holds several levels, and ANOVA asks whether the response mean differs across those levels.**

## What ANOVA is really asking

```python
import pandas as pd

# ANOVA data lives in "tidy" form: one factor column (the levels)
# and one response column. Here: medicine dosage (factor) vs outcome.
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "dosage": ["5mg", "5mg", "10mg", "10mg", "15mg", "15mg"],   # factor -> its levels
    "response": [12, 14, 18, 17, 23, 25],                        # what we measure
})

# ANOVA asks: does mean(response) differ ACROSS the dosage levels?
print(df.groupby("dosage")["response"].mean())
# The F-test that answers it (stats.f_oneway / statsmodels) comes once we've
# covered the assumptions, types, and how the variance is partitioned.
```

## Why a quant cares

"Compare the means of several groups" is a question that shows up constantly once you stop thinking in pairs:

- **Strategy across regimes.** Is a strategy's mean return the same in low-, medium-, and high-volatility regimes (three levels of a "regime" factor), or does at least one differ? That's a one-factor ANOVA, not three separate t-tests.
- **A/B/C/… testing.** Comparing more than two execution algos, venues, or signal variants at once is exactly ANOVA's turf — and doing it as one test controls the false-positive rate that many pairwise comparisons would blow up.
- **The factor/level frame organizes the data.** Recognizing "this column is a factor, these are its levels, that column is the response" is the tidy-data layout every group-comparison model (ANOVA, and the [regression / GLM](/docs/Statistics/regression-analysis) it's equivalent to) consumes.

We'll build the machinery — assumptions, types, and the variance partition that yields the F-statistic — over the next lessons.
