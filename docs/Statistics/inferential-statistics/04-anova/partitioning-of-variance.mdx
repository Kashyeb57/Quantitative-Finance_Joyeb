# Partitioning of Variance in ANOVA

This is where [ANOVA](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/anova/anova-test) finally gets *computed*. We've defined it (comparing the means of two or more groups), listed its [assumptions](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/anova/assumptions-of-anova), and named its [three types](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/anova/types-of-anova). Now we perform the hypothesis test step by step — a process also called **partitioning the variance**, because the whole trick is splitting the total variation into a *between-groups* piece and a *within-groups* piece and comparing them.

## The hypotheses

ANOVA compares the means of two or more groups, so the **null hypothesis** says every group mean is the same:

$$
H_0:\ \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3 = \cdots = \mu_k
$$

The **alternate hypothesis** is where people slip. It is simply:

$$
H_1:\ \text{at least one of the sample means is not equal}
$$

:::warning Don't write H₁ as μ₁ ≠ μ₂ ≠ μ₃ ≠ ⋯
It's tempting to write the alternate as $\mu_1 \neq \mu_2 \neq \mu_3 \neq \cdots \neq \mu_k$ — but that's a **stronger, wrong** claim. That notation insists **every** mean differs from **every** other. ANOVA only needs **at least one** mean to stand apart from the rest. So we say it in words: *at least one of the sample means is not equal.*
:::

## The test statistic: the F-test

The statistic ANOVA uses is the **F-test**, and its shape is beautifully simple:

$$
F = \frac{\text{variance between samples}}{\text{variance within samples}}
$$

The formula is short; the work behind it is not. Getting those two variances takes a lot of small steps — so we'll walk them carefully. First, what do "between" and "within" actually mean? Take three samples:

| $X_1$ | $X_2$ | $X_3$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 5 |
| 2 | 7 | 6 |
| 4 | 3 | 3 |
| 5 | 2 | 2 |
| 3 | 1 | 4 |
| **x̄₁ = 3** | **x̄₂ = 3.8** | **x̄₃ = 4** |

- **Variance *between* samples** — how far the **group means** ($\bar x_1, \bar x_2, \bar x_3$) sit from one another (equivalently, from the overall grand mean). This is the *signal*: real differences between the groups.
- **Variance *within* samples** — inside a single group, how far its points scatter from **their own** group mean. In $X_1$ the mean is 3, but 1 and 2 sit below it while 4 and 5 sit above — that spread around 3 is the within-group variance. This is the *noise*.

F is signal over noise. If the gaps **between** the groups are large relative to the scatter **within** them, F is large and the group means are probably genuinely different. Slide the two knobs below and watch F respond:

## A worked one-way ANOVA, step by step

This is the biggest test in the module — heavy on arithmetic, not on code. Follow the six steps once and it clicks.

> A **doctor wants to test a new medication that reduces headache.** Participants are split into **three conditions — 15 mg, 30 mg, 45 mg** (the dosage is the [factor's levels](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/anova/anova-test)). Each patient then **rates their headache from 1 to 10** (10 = headache completely gone). **Are there differences between the three conditions, using α = 0.05?**

Seven patients per dose:

| Patient | 15 mg | 30 mg | 45 mg |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 9 | 7 | 4 |
| 2 | 8 | 6 | 3 |
| 3 | 7 | 6 | 2 |
| 4 | 8 | 7 | 3 |
| 5 | 8 | 8 | 4 |
| 6 | 9 | 7 | 3 |
| 7 | 8 | 6 | 2 |
| **Σ** | **57** | **47** | **21** |

### Step 1 — Null and alternate hypotheses

$$
H_0:\ \mu_{15} = \mu_{30} = \mu_{45} \qquad H_1:\ \text{not all means are equal}
$$

### Step 2 — Significance level

$$
\alpha = 0.05 \qquad \text{confidence interval} = 0.95
$$

### Step 3 — Degrees of freedom

Three counts drive everything. The **total sample size** $N = 7 \times 3 = 21$; the **per-group size** $n = 7$; and $a =$ the **number of categories** (groups) $= 3$.

$$
df_{\text{between}} = a - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2
$$
$$
df_{\text{within}} = N - a = 21 - 3 = 18
$$
$$
df_{\text{total}} = N - 1 = 21 - 1 = 20
$$

Notice $df_{\text{between}} + df_{\text{within}} = 2 + 18 = 20 = df_{\text{total}}$ — the degrees of freedom partition just like the variance will. The pair we carry forward is $(d_1, d_2) = (2, 18)$: the two numbers you look up in the **F table**.

### Step 4 — Decision boundary

The F-distribution is **right-skewed** (its tail is stretched to the right), so an ANOVA is **one-tailed**: the entire rejection region — area $\alpha = 0.05$ — is the right tail, leaving the $0.95$ acceptance region to its left. Looking up the F table at $d_1 = 2$, $d_2 = 18$, $\alpha = 0.05$ gives the **critical value**:

$$
\text{CV} = F_{2,18,\,0.05} = 3.5546
$$

**Decision rule:** *if the computed F is greater than 3.5546, reject the null hypothesis.*

### Step 5 — Compute the F-test statistic

We build the ANOVA table one column at a time: **SS** (sum of squares) → **df** → **MS** (mean square) → **F**.

**① Sum of squares between groups.** With $\sum a_i$ = each group's total (57, 47, 21) and $T = \sum(\sum a_i) = 125$ the grand total:

$$
SS_{\text{between}} = \frac{\sum \left(\sum a_i\right)^2}{n} - \frac{T^2}{N}
= \frac{57^2 + 47^2 + 21^2}{7} - \frac{125^2}{21}
= 842.71 - 744.05 = 98.67
$$

**② Sum of squares within groups.** Here $\sum y^2$ squares **every** raw value and adds them ($9^2 + 8^2 + 7^2 + \cdots = 853$):

$$
SS_{\text{within}} = \sum y^2 - \frac{\sum \left(\sum a_i\right)^2}{n}
= 853 - \frac{57^2 + 47^2 + 21^2}{7} = 853 - 842.71 = 10.29
$$

**③ Total** is just the two added (the partition):

$$
SS_{\text{total}} = SS_{\text{between}} + SS_{\text{within}} = 98.67 + 10.29 = 108.96
$$

Now **mean square = SS ÷ df**, and finally **F = MS between ÷ MS within**:

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Between** | 98.67 | 2 | 98.67 / 2 = **49.34** | 49.34 / 0.57 = **86.56** |
| **Within** | 10.29 | 18 | 10.29 / 18 = **0.57** | |
| **Total** | 108.96 | 20 | | |

$$
F = \frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}} = \frac{49.34}{0.57} = 86.56
$$

(MS within is $10.29/18 = 0.571\ldots$, which rounds to $0.57$; carrying more decimals gives $F \approx 86.3$ — either way, enormous.)

### Step 6 — Decision

$$
86.56 > 3.5546 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{reject } H_0
$$

F lands **far** past the critical value — deep in the right tail (p-value ≈ $6\times10^{-10}$). **Conclusion: there *are* differences between the three conditions.** Not all the dosage means are equal — at least one differs from the others. Higher doses really do change the headache rating.

```python
from scipy import stats

g15 = [9, 8, 7, 8, 8, 9, 8]
g30 = [7, 6, 6, 7, 8, 7, 6]
g45 = [4, 3, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2]

F, p = stats.f_oneway(g15, g30, g45)
print(f"F = {F:.2f},  p = {p:.2e}")
# F ≈ 86.3,  p ≈ 6e-10  — matches the by-hand F (small gaps are just rounding)

# Critical value from the F table, α = 0.05, df = (a-1, N-a) = (2, 18):
crit = stats.f.ppf(0.95, dfn=2, dfd=18)
print(f"critical F(2,18) = {crit:.4f}")   # 3.5546
print("reject H0" if F > crit else "fail to reject H0")
```

## Why a quant cares

Partitioning variance into *between* and *within* is not just an ANOVA ritual — it's the same decomposition that runs underneath most of quantitative finance:

- **Signal-to-noise is the whole game.** F = between ÷ within is literally *effect size ÷ dispersion* — the same shape as a **Sharpe ratio** (mean edge ÷ volatility) or an **information ratio**. A strategy's mean return can look different across regimes, but if the *within-regime* scatter dwarfs the *between-regime* gap, the difference is noise. ANOVA formalizes exactly that judgement.
- **Variance decomposition is everywhere.** The $SS_{\text{total}} = SS_{\text{between}} + SS_{\text{within}}$ split is the ANOVA face of the [regression](/docs/Statistics/regression-analysis) identity *explained + residual variance*, and of risk attribution (systematic vs idiosyncratic). Once you see variance as something you **partition** rather than just measure, factor models and performance attribution read the same way.
- **F over a table of p-values.** Testing three strategies (or venues, or signals) with one F-test controls the false-positive rate that many pairwise t-tests would inflate — the same discipline that keeps a research pipeline from data-mining spurious "winners."

The mechanics are heavy, but the idea is one line: **real differences between groups are only convincing when they overwhelm the randomness inside them.**
