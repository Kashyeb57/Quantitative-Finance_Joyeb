# Bayes' Statistics (Bayes' Theorem)

**Bayesian statistics is an approach to data analysis and parameter estimation based on Bayes' theorem.** Where the [hypothesis tests](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/foundations/hypothesis-testing-mechanism) so far ask *"how surprising is this data if the null is true?"*, Bayes flips the question to the one you actually care about: **"given the data, how probable is my hypothesis?"** To get there we start from the two flavours of probability.

## Independent vs dependent events

**① Independent events** — one outcome tells you nothing about the next.

- **Rolling a die** over $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$: each face has $P(1) = P(2) = \dots = \tfrac{1}{6}$, and the previous roll doesn't change the next.
- **Tossing a coin**: $P(H) = 0.5,\ P(T) = 0.5$, every toss fresh.

**② Dependent events** — one outcome *changes* the probability of the next. Draw balls from a box **without replacing them** and the mix shifts as you go. Say the chance of drawing **red** is $P(R) = \tfrac{2}{5}$, and then — with one ball already gone — the chance of **yellow** becomes $P(Y \mid R) = \tfrac{3}{4}$. That second term is a **conditional probability**: the probability of $Y$ *given that* $R$ already happened.

## Conditional probability and the multiplication rule

The probability of **both** dependent events is the first times the second-given-the-first:

$$
P(R \text{ and } Y) = P(R)\cdot \underbrace{P(Y \mid R)}_{\text{conditional}} = \frac{2}{5}\cdot\frac{3}{4} = \frac{6}{20}
$$

## Deriving Bayes' theorem

Here's the one clever step. The probability of "$A$ and $B$" can't depend on the order you name them:

$$
P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B \text{ and } A)
$$

Write each side with the multiplication rule:

$$
P(A)\cdot P(B \mid A) = P(B)\cdot P(A \mid B)
$$

Now just divide to isolate the conditional you want. Solving for $P(B \mid A)$:

$$
P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(B)\cdot P(A \mid B)}{P(A)}
$$

or, the form we'll use most, solving for $P(A \mid B)$:

$$
\boxed{\,P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A)\cdot P(B \mid A)}{P(B)}\,} \qquad\Leftarrow\ \textbf{Bayes' theorem}
$$

Reading each term, where $A, B$ are events:

- $P(A \mid B)$ — probability of **$A$ given $B$ is true** (the **posterior** — what we want).
- $P(B \mid A)$ — probability of **$B$ given $A$ is true** (the **likelihood**).
- $P(A)$ — the standalone probability of $A$ (the **prior**).
- $P(B)$ — the standalone probability of $B$ (the **evidence**, a normalizer).

## The base-rate trap — see it

Bayes matters most when your intuition fails, and it fails hardest on **rare events.** A test can be "90% accurate" and yet a positive result still mean you're *probably fine* — because a small **prior** $P(A)$ drags the **posterior** $P(A\mid B)$ far below the test's accuracy. ## From theorem to classifier: Naive Bayes

Bayes' theorem is also the engine of a real machine-learning model. Take a **house-price dataset** with **independent** features — size of house $x_1$, number of rooms $x_2$, location $x_3$ — predicting a **dependent output** $y$ (the price). Bayes gives the probability of an output given the features:

$$
P(y \mid x_1, x_2, x_3) = \frac{P(y)\cdot P(x_1, x_2, x_3 \mid y)}{P(x_1, x_2, x_3)}
$$

Assume the features are (naively) independent given $y$ and this becomes the **Naive Bayes classifier** — cheap, surprisingly strong, and the classic baseline for spam filtering and text classification.

```python
# Bayes' theorem: the base-rate trap in numbers.
# A disease affects 1% of people. A test is 90% sensitive with a 9% false-positive rate.
# You test positive — what's the chance you actually have it?
prior = 0.01          # P(A): base rate
sens  = 0.90          # P(B|A): true-positive rate
fpr   = 0.09          # P(B|¬A): false-positive rate

p_B = prior*sens + (1 - prior)*fpr          # P(B): total prob. of a positive test
posterior = prior*sens / p_B                 # P(A|B): Bayes' theorem

print(f"P(positive test)          = {p_B:.3f}")
print(f"P(disease | positive) = {posterior:.3f}")   # ≈ 0.092 — only ~9%, not 90%!
```

## Why a quant cares

Bayes is the native language of decision-making under uncertainty, which is the whole job:

- **Updating beliefs with evidence.** Start with a **prior** on a parameter (a stock's true drift, a regime probability), see data, and compute a **posterior**. That's Bayesian estimation — and the [Bayesian Inference](/docs/Statistics/bayesian-inference) page has a prior→posterior updater you can drag.
- **The base-rate trap is a risk trap.** "This alert is 95% accurate" is meaningless without the base rate of the thing it detects. Rare-event detectors — fraud, default, blow-up — are swamped by false positives exactly as the disease test is; ignoring the prior is how teams drown in false alarms or trust a bad signal.
- **A credible interval says what people *think* a confidence interval says.** The Bayesian posterior supports the direct statement "95% probability the parameter is in this range" — the honest version of the frequentist [confidence interval](/docs/Statistics/inferential-statistics/foundations/big-picture), and the basis of the ROPE decision rule.
