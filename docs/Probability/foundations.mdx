# Probability Foundations

**Probability is about determining the likelihood of an event.** It's the native language of quantitative finance: a price isn't a number you predict, it's a random outcome you assign probabilities to. This page builds the ground floor — what a probability *is*, and the first rule for combining events: the **addition rule** for "or."

## What is a probability?

A probability is a number between **0 (impossible)** and **1 (certain)** measuring how likely an event is. When every outcome is **equally likely**, it's just a ratio:

$$
P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}
$$

The set of all possible outcomes is the **sample space**; an **event** is any subset of it.

- **Toss a coin** — sample space $\{H, T\}$. $\;P(H) = \tfrac{1}{2} = 50\%$, $\;P(T) = \tfrac{1}{2} = 50\%$.
- **Roll a die** — sample space $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. $\;P(X=1) = \tfrac{1}{6}$.

A few rules follow immediately and are worth stating:

- $0 \le P(A) \le 1$ for any event $A$.
- The probabilities of the whole sample space sum to $1$.
- **Complement:** $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$ — the chance something *doesn't* happen.

## What a probability *means*: four interpretations

The ratio above is one *interpretation* of probability — but not the only one, and which you hold quietly shapes how you model. Four stances have competed for centuries:

- **Classical** — probabilities come from **symmetry**: $n$ equally likely outcomes each get $1/n$, exactly the ratio above. Clean for dice and cards, but silent the moment outcomes are not symmetric (there is no "equally likely" split for a stock going up).
- **Frequency** — a probability is the **long-run relative frequency** of an event over infinitely many repeatable trials. This is the interpretation behind classical (frequentist) statistics — and, as the [Law of Large Numbers](/docs/Probability/distributions/sampling-and-estimation/law-of-large-numbers) makes precise, that relative frequency really does converge to the probability. It strains, though, for one-shot events: there is no ensemble of *this Tuesday's Fed decision*.
- **Logical / epistemic** — probability measures the **degree to which evidence supports** a proposition, aspiring to be as objective as logic.
- **Subjective (Bayesian)** — a probability is a rational agent's **degree of belief**, calibrated so the bets you would accept stay consistent (no one can build a sure-loss *Dutch book* against you). It applies to *any* proposition, repeatable or not — which is why it fits markets, and why it is the foundation of [Bayesian inference](/docs/Statistics/bayesian-inference).

These are not merely philosophy: the frequency view underwrites confidence intervals and p-values, while the subjective view underwrites priors and posteriors. Most working quants are pragmatic — frequentist for a quick test, Bayesian when they must fold in prior knowledge.

## Mutually exclusive events

**Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot occur at the same time.** On a single coin toss you get heads *or* tails, never both — $H$ and $T$ are mutually exclusive.

For mutually exclusive events, the **addition rule** is as simple as it looks: the chance of one *or* the other is the sum.

$$
P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)
$$

- **Coin:** $P(H \text{ or } T) = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ — one of them is certain to happen.
- **Die:** $P(1 \text{ or } 5) = \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{2}{6} = \tfrac{1}{3}$.

## Non-mutually-exclusive events: the general addition rule

Most interesting events *can* happen together, and then simple addition **double-counts** the overlap. The classic example: draw one card from a 52-card deck.

- Event $K$ = "it's a King" — 4 cards, $P(K) = \tfrac{4}{52}$.
- Event $\heartsuit$ = "it's a Heart" — 13 cards, $P(\heartsuit) = \tfrac{13}{52}$.

These overlap: the **King of Hearts** is both. If you just add $\tfrac{4}{52} + \tfrac{13}{52}$ you count that one card *twice*. The fix is to subtract the overlap once — the **general addition rule** (also called **inclusion–exclusion**):

$$
P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)
$$

$$
P(K \text{ or } \heartsuit) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{13}{52} - \frac{1}{52} = \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13}
$$

Mutually exclusive events are just the special case where $P(A \text{ and } B) = 0$, and the overlap term vanishes — which is why the simpler rule above worked for coins and dice.

```python
# P(King OR Heart) from a 52-card deck, via the addition rule
kings          = 4/52
hearts         = 13/52
king_of_hearts = 1/52          # the overlap: King AND Heart

naive   = kings + hearts                     # WRONG: double-counts the King of Hearts
correct = kings + hearts - king_of_hearts    # inclusion-exclusion

print(f"just adding:        {naive:.4f}  = {round(naive*52)}/52")
print(f"subtract overlap:   {correct:.4f}  = {round(correct*52)}/52  (correct)")
```

## Why a quant cares

Every question in markets is really about the probability of an event — $P(\text{up day})$, $P(\text{price breaches a barrier})$, $P(\text{default this year})$. Two of these foundations matter more than they first appear:

- **The overlap term is where risk hides.** You *cannot* get $P(\text{stock A falls OR stock B falls})$ by adding the two probabilities — they overlap (both can fall together), and ignoring $P(\text{A and B})$ **overstates** the chance of "at least one." How big that overlap is comes straight from how the two are related — the [covariance and correlation](/docs/Statistics/descriptive-statistics/covariance-and-correlation/covariance) from the Statistics section.
- **The complement rule is a constant shortcut.** $P(\text{at least one loss}) = 1 - P(\text{no losses})$ is almost always easier to compute than the "at least one" side directly.

Next, the [addition rule's partner](/docs/Probability/conditional-probability) — the **multiplication rule** for "and," and the all-important question of whether two events are *independent*.
