# Pareto Distribution

The **Pareto distribution** is the named, formal probability distribution behind the [power law](/docs/Probability/distributions/heavy-tails/power-law) and the 80–20 rule. It is a decidedly **non-Gaussian** distribution — the opposite of the tidy bell.

**Named after Vilfredo Pareto — the Italian civil engineer, economist, and sociologist — it is a power-law probability distribution used to describe social, quality-control, scientific, geophysical, actuarial, and many other observable phenomena.** The principle was originally applied to the **distribution of wealth in a society**, fitting the trend that **a large portion of wealth is held by a small fraction of the population**.

## The shape and the tail index α

The Pareto (Type I) has a minimum value $x_m$ (the scale) and a shape parameter $\alpha$ (the **tail index**):

$$
f(x) = \frac{\alpha\, x_m^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha+1}}, \quad x \ge x_m, \qquad F(x) = 1 - \left(\frac{x_m}{x}\right)^{\alpha}
$$

Everything hinges on $\alpha$. **As $\alpha$ increases the tail gets lighter** and the distribution crowds toward $x_m$; as $\alpha$ *decreases* the tail gets **heavier** and rare-but-huge values dominate. The lecture shows Pareto Type I for $\alpha = 1, 2, 3, \infty$ — and $\alpha = \infty$ is the limit where the whole distribution collapses onto a spike at $x_m$.

Slide $\alpha$ and watch the tail — note the mean readout goes to $\infty$ once $\alpha \le 1$:

<PDFExplorer kind="pareto" title="Pareto density — α is the tail index (lower α = heavier tail)" />

The tail index has hard thresholds that matter enormously:

$$
\alpha \le 1 \Rightarrow \text{mean is infinite}, \qquad \alpha \le 2 \Rightarrow \text{variance is infinite}
$$

$$
\text{Mean} = \frac{\alpha\, x_m}{\alpha - 1} \quad (\text{for } \alpha > 1)
$$

An infinite variance isn't a curiosity — it means the usual $\sigma$-based risk numbers **don't exist**, and any sample estimate of them is meaningless.

## Transforming a Pareto

Like any power law, a Pareto is hostile to normal-based methods — so you **transform it back toward normal** before modeling:

$$
\text{Pareto} \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{transformation}\;}\; \text{Log-Normal} \;\xrightarrow{\text{Box–Cox}}\; \text{Normal}
$$

Taking logs turns the multiplicative Pareto tail into something [log-normal](/docs/Probability/distributions/continuous/log-normal)-like, and a **Box–Cox transform** finishes the job so a [QQ plot](/docs/Probability/distributions/continuous/normal) straightens out.

## The Pareto principle in practice

The 80–20 principle applied to the **IT industry**:

1. **80% of the entire project is done by 20% of the team.**
2. **80% of the defects can be solved if we solve 20% of the defects** — fix the vital few and most of the problem disappears.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

xm, alpha = 1.0, 1.16                       # α≈1.16 -> the classic 80-20
x = (rng.pareto(alpha, size=200_000) + 1) * xm
x.sort()

top20 = x[int(0.8*len(x)):].sum() / x.sum()
print(f"top 20% hold {top20*100:.0f}% of the total")     # ~80%

for a in (0.9, 1.5, 3.0):
    mean = a*xm/(a-1) if a > 1 else float('inf')
    print(f"α={a}: mean = {mean}"
          + ("  (infinite!)" if a <= 1 else "")
          + ("  (infinite variance)" if a <= 2 else ""))
```

## Why a quant cares

The Pareto is the working model wherever **a few observations carry most of the risk**:

- **Insurance & actuarial science** model **large claims** with a Pareto — the handful of catastrophic payouts, not the many small ones, determine solvency. This is textbook **Extreme Value Theory (EVT)**, which fits a Pareto to the tail beyond a high threshold.
- **Operational and credit risk** — rare, huge losses (rogue trades, fraud, systemic defaults) live in a Pareto tail; Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall in the tail are estimated with Pareto/EVT fits, not the normal.
- **The tail index α *is* a risk number.** Estimating $\alpha$ for asset-return tails tells you whether variance even exists ($\alpha < 2$) — a sharper warning than any volatility figure. Empirically, many return tails sit near $\alpha \approx 3$, heavy enough to make "$\sigma$-based" risk dangerously optimistic.

Where the normal says "this can't happen," the Pareto says "this is how you go bankrupt." Modeling the tail with the right distribution is the whole difference.
