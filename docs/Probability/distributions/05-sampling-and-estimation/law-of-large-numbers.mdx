# Law of Large Numbers

The **Law of Large Numbers (LLN)** is the theorem that makes averages trustworthy: as you collect more independent observations, their **sample average converges to the true mean** $\mu$. It is why a casino profits with near-certainty over millions of bets while any single spin is a coin-flip — and why a Monte Carlo estimate creeps closer to the right answer the longer you run it.

## The statement

Take independent draws $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ from a distribution with mean $\mu$, and form the running average

$$
\bar{x}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i .
$$

The LLN says that as $n \to \infty$, $\bar{x}_n \to \mu$. It comes in two strengths:

- **Weak law** — for any tolerance $\varepsilon$, the probability that the average is more than $\varepsilon$ from $\mu$ goes to zero: $P(|\bar{x}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \to 0$. (Convergence *in probability*.)
- **Strong law** — the running average *itself* settles onto $\mu$ with probability 1: $P(\lim_{n\to\infty} \bar{x}_n = \mu) = 1$. (Almost-sure convergence — the stronger "it will actually lock on" guarantee.)

For everyday purposes they say the same practical thing: **average enough independent draws and you land on the true mean.**

## See it happen

Watch a running average of fair dice rolls (true mean $\mu = 3.5$) settle as the sample grows — jumpy at first, then pinned:

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=100_000)          # fair six-sided die, μ = 3.5
running_avg = np.cumsum(rolls) / np.arange(1, len(rolls) + 1)

for n in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000]:
    print(f"n = {n:>7,}:  running average = {running_avg[n-1]:.4f}   (true μ = 3.5)")
```

Early on the average swings widely; by $n = 100{,}000$ it sits within a whisker of 3.5. Notice it does **not** snap to 3.5 and freeze — it wanders in, with the size of the wandering shrinking like $1/\sqrt{n}$.

## LLN is not the CLT

These two theorems are constantly confused. They answer different questions about the same $\bar{x}_n$:

- **LLN — *where* it goes.** The average converges to $\mu$. A statement about the **center**.
- **[Central Limit Theorem](/docs/Probability/distributions/sampling-and-estimation/central-limit-theorem) — *how* it's spread.** For finite $n$, the average is approximately $N(\mu,\, \sigma/\sqrt{n})$. A statement about the **shape and the error**.

The LLN promises you arrive; the CLT tells you how far off you still are after $n$ draws (the standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$). Both share one fine-print assumption: a **finite mean** and **independence**. In fat-tailed markets a mean can be effectively unstable and bets can be correlated — which is exactly when "just average more" quietly fails.

## Why a quant cares

- **Monte Carlo pricing *is* the LLN.** Estimating an option price as the average payoff over simulated paths works because that average converges to the true expectation; the CLT then sizes the error ($\propto 1/\sqrt{n}$).
- **Edge only shows up in the long run.** A strategy with a small positive expectancy is indistinguishable from noise over ten trades and near-certain over ten thousand — *provided the trades are roughly independent*. Position sizing exists to keep you in the game long enough for the LLN to pay out.
- **The gambler's fallacy is the LLN misread.** Convergence of the *average* does not mean the next draw "corrects" a losing streak — the dice have no memory. The average is dragged toward $\mu$ by accumulating fresh draws that dilute early deviations, not by reversals.
