# Central Limit Theorem

The **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)** is the reason statistics works at all. It rests on one concept — the **sampling distribution** — and delivers one astonishing result.

## The idea: a sampling distribution

**A sampling distribution is the probability distribution of a *statistic* computed over a large number of samples taken from a population.** Here the statistic is the **mean**. The procedure:

1. Start with a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$.
2. Draw a sample of size $n$ and compute its mean — call it $\bar{x}_1$.
3. Draw another sample of size $n$, get $\bar{x}_2$. And another, $\bar{x}_3$… up to $\bar{x}_m$.

$$
S_1 = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\} \to \bar{x}_1, \quad S_2 = \{x_2, x_3, \dots, x_n\} \to \bar{x}_2, \quad \dots, \quad S_m \to \bar{x}_m
$$

Those sample means $\{\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2, \dots, \bar{x}_m\}$ form a distribution of their own — the **sampling distribution of the mean**, centered on $\mu_{\bar{x}}$.

## The theorem

> **The sampling distribution of the mean will always be normally distributed, as long as the sample size is large enough — regardless of whether the population is normal, Poisson, binomial, or any other distribution.**

That's the magic. It doesn't matter what shape you start from:

- **① If the population *is* normal**, $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)$ — the sample means are normal too (no surprise).
- **② If the population is *not* normal** — skewed, bimodal, exponential, anything — the sample means **still** pile up into a normal, provided the sample size is large enough. The rule of thumb is:

$$
\boxed{\,n \ge 30 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{sampling distribution of the mean is} \approx \text{Normal}\,}
$$

## The result in a formula

The sampling distribution of the mean isn't just *normal* — we know its exact parameters. Starting from a normal population $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)$:

$$
\bar{X} \sim N\!\left(\mu,\; \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
$$

where $\sigma$ = population standard deviation, $\mu$ = population mean, $n$ = sample size. Two facts fall out:

1. **The mean of the sample means is the population mean**, $\mu_{\bar{x}} = \mu$ — averaging is unbiased.
2. **The spread shrinks as $\sqrt{n}$.** The standard deviation of the sample mean — called the **standard error** — is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. More data ⇒ a tighter estimate, but only at the rate $\sqrt{n}$: to halve your error you need **four times** the data.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# a strongly NON-normal population: exponential (right-skewed)
mu, sigma = 1.0, 1.0                       # exponential(1): mean=1, std=1
n, m = 30, 20_000
means = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(m, n)).mean(axis=1)   # m sample means

print(f"population: mean={mu}, std={sigma}  (very skewed)")
print(f"sample means: mean={means.mean():.3f}  (≈ μ)")
print(f"std of sample means = {means.std():.3f}")
print(f"theory σ/√n         = {sigma/np.sqrt(n):.3f}")   # they match
```

## Why a quant cares

The CLT is the licence to use the normal *even when the data isn't normal* — and it quietly underwrites most of finance:

- **Averages are trustworthy; single observations aren't.** One day's return is drawn from a fat-tailed mess. The *average* of many is approximately normal, so its uncertainty is governed by $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. This is why you estimate an expected return or a Sharpe ratio from a long track record, not one trade.
- **The $\sqrt{n}$ law is everywhere.** The standard error of a mean, the precision of a **Monte Carlo** price (error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$), the [√252 volatility annualization](/docs/Statistics/descriptive-statistics/summary-measures/standard-deviation) — all the same theorem. It's also why more data helps, but with diminishing returns.
- **Diversification.** A portfolio of many roughly-independent bets has a P&L that's the *average* of the individual ones — pushed toward normal and with spread cut by $\sqrt{n}$. (The catch, as always: the CLT assumes independence. Correlated bets don't diversify, and their tails stay fat — the [power-law](/docs/Probability/distributions/heavy-tails/power-law) world.)

The CLT is also the bridge to the next lesson: because $\bar{x}$ is normal with a known standard error, we can turn a sample mean into an **[estimate](/docs/Probability/distributions/sampling-and-estimation/estimates)** — a point, and an interval around it.
