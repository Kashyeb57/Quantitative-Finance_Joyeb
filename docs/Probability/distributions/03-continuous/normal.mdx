# Normal / Gaussian Distribution

**The normal distribution (also called the Gaussian distribution) is a continuous probability distribution for a real-valued random variable.** It's the famous **bell curve** — the single most important distribution in statistics and the one most models quietly *assume*.

Its defining features:

1. It's a **continuous** random variable (a **PDF**), with $X \in \mathbb{R}$ — $X = \{\dots\}$ smeared across the whole number line.
2. It's a **symmetric** distribution — a bell curve, the same on both sides of the center.
3. Because it's symmetric and single-peaked, **mean = median = mode** all sit at the center.
4. The spread is set by the variance: as $\sigma^2 \uparrow$, the **spread $\uparrow$** — the bell gets wider and flatter.

**Examples:** the weights of students in a class; the heights of students in a class; the four measurements in the classic **IRIS dataset** (petal length, sepal length, petal width, sepal width) — the kind of naturally-occurring measurements researchers find are approximately normal.

## Notation, parameters, PDF

$$
X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

- **Parameters:** $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ = the **mean** (location); $\;\sigma^2 \in \mathbb{R}, > 0$ = the **variance** (spread); and $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

The density itself:

$$
\text{PDF} = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\; e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}}
$$

The mean and variance are computed exactly as in [descriptive statistics](/docs/Statistics/descriptive-statistics/summary-measures/central-tendency):

$$
\mu = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{x_i}{n}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{n}, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}
$$

Formulas don't build intuition — sliders do. **Slide $\mu$** and the whole curve shifts without changing shape (pure location); **crank $\sigma$** and it flattens and spreads. The shaded area between $a$ and $b$ **is** the probability:

## The empirical rule: 68–95–99.7

Because the shape is fixed, the probability within a given number of standard deviations of the mean is **always the same**, whatever $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are. This is the **empirical rule** (turn on the bands in the explorer to see it):

$$
P(\mu - \sigma \le X \le \mu + \sigma) \approx 68\% \quad(68.27\%)
$$

$$
P(\mu - 2\sigma \le X \le \mu + 2\sigma) \approx 95\% \quad(95.45\%)
$$

$$
P(\mu - 3\sigma \le X \le \mu + 3\sigma) \approx 99.7\% \quad(99.73\%)
$$

So ~68% of the mass is within one σ, ~95% within two, ~99.7% within three. A move beyond 3σ should happen only about 0.3% of the time — which is exactly why, when markets produce them monthly, you learn something is wrong with the normal assumption.

A quick way to *check* whether data is actually normal is a **QQ plot** (quantile–quantile plot): plot the data's quantiles against a normal's. If the points fall on a straight line, the data is normal; systematic curls at the ends reveal skew or fat tails. We'll use QQ plots repeatedly in the [heavy-tails module](/docs/Probability/distributions/heavy-tails/power-law).

Correlation reshapes this hill: at $\rho = 0$ it's the round bell above; as $\rho \to \pm 1$ it collapses into a **ridge** along the diagonal — the geometry of two variables that move together. There's a rotatable $\rho = 0.8$ version on the [Correlation page](/docs/Statistics/descriptive-statistics/covariance-and-correlation/correlation).

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(0, 1, size=1_000_000)   # standard normal

for k in (1, 2, 3):
    inside = np.mean(np.abs(x) <= k)
    print(f"within ±{k}σ: {inside*100:.2f}%")   # ≈ 68.27, 95.45, 99.73
```

## Why a quant cares

The normal is the **default assumption** across finance — and both halves of that sentence matter:

- **Where it's relied on:** returns models, the Black–Scholes option-pricing framework (log-returns normal), portfolio theory (mean–variance), confidence intervals, and Value-at-Risk all lean on normality because it's analytically clean — a whole model collapses to $\mu$ and $\sigma$.
- **Where it fails:** real daily returns are **not** normal. They're more **peaked** in the middle (lots of tiny days) and have **fatter tails** (extreme days far more often than 68–95–99.7 allows). A "3-sigma" crash should be a once-a-century event under the normal; markets serve them up every few years. Assuming normality therefore **understates crash risk** — the motivation for the [log-normal](/docs/Probability/distributions/continuous/log-normal) and [power-law / Pareto](/docs/Probability/distributions/heavy-tails/pareto) models later in this module.

The normal is the map; fat tails are the territory. Know the map cold, then never forget it's only a map.
