# Log-Normal Distribution

**A log-normal (or lognormal) distribution is a continuous probability distribution of a random variable whose *logarithm* is normally distributed.** That one sentence is the whole idea:

$$
\text{If } X \sim \text{Log-Normal}(\mu, \sigma), \quad\text{then}\quad Y = \ln(X) \sim \text{Normal}
$$

and equivalently, going the other way,

$$
\text{If } Y \sim \text{Normal}, \quad\text{then}\quad X = e^{Y} \sim \text{Log-Normal}
$$

Take a normal variable and **exponentiate** it and you get a log-normal; take a log-normal and apply the **natural log** ($\log_e$) and you get back a normal. The two are the same information in two coordinate systems.

## The shape: right-skewed and positive-only

Because $X = e^{Y}$ and an exponential is always positive, a log-normal variable is **strictly positive** ($X > 0$) — it can never be negative. And it's **right-skewed**: a fat hump near the low end with a long tail stretching to the right. Increasing $\sigma$ (the spread of the *underlying* normal) makes the skew more extreme.

Notice the readout: the **median $e^{\mu}$ sits to the *left* of the mean** $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. That gap between median and mean *is* right-skew — the long right tail drags the average above the typical value.

## Turning it back into a normal

The defining relationship is also a *tool*: if data looks log-normal, **take logs and it becomes normal** — after which every normal-based method (z-scores, confidence intervals, the empirical rule) applies. A [QQ plot](/docs/Probability/distributions/continuous/normal) of $\ln(X)$ against a normal should fall on a straight line. (The more general version of this trick — a **Box–Cox transform** — appears in the [power-law lesson](/docs/Probability/distributions/heavy-tails/power-law).)

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

Y = rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=200_000)   # underlying normal
X = np.exp(Y)                          # -> log-normal

def skew(v):                            # simple sample skewness
    z = (v - v.mean()) / v.std()
    return (z**3).mean()

print(f"X (log-normal):  skew = {skew(X):+.2f}  (right-skewed, > 0)")
print(f"ln(X) = Y:       skew = {skew(np.log(X)):+.2f}  (~0, back to normal)")
print(f"median e^μ = {np.exp(0):.2f}   <   mean e^(μ+σ²/2) = {np.exp(0 + 0.5**2/2):.2f}")
```

## Where log-normals show up

The lecture's examples are all quantities that are **positive and right-skewed** — a crowd near the low end and a few giants in the tail:

1. **Wealth distribution of the world** — most people salaried near the peak, a long tail out to Bill Gates, Adani, Elon Musk.
2. **Discussion forums** — the length of comments (many short, a few essays).
3. **Length of chess games** (most games a typical length, some marathons).
4. **Dwell time on online articles** (jokes, news) — most readers skim, a few linger.
5. **Salaries of employees in a company.**

The common thread: a hard floor at zero, a typical range, and a rare-but-huge upper tail.

## Why a quant cares

**The log-normal is the distribution of prices.** It's not a side-note — it's the backbone of continuous-time finance:

- **Stock prices are modeled as log-normal.** The Black–Scholes model assumes *log-returns* are normal, which makes the *price* log-normal. This is exactly right for two reasons: prices can't go negative (log-normal is positive-only), and returns **compound multiplicatively** — a price is a product of many small growth factors, and the log of a product is a sum, which the [Central Limit Theorem](/docs/Probability/distributions/sampling-and-estimation/central-limit-theorem) pushes toward normal. So $\ln(\text{price})$ is normal ⇒ price is log-normal.
- **Geometric Brownian Motion**, the standard price process, produces log-normally distributed prices at every horizon — see [stochastic processes](/docs/Probability/stochastic-processes).
- **The median-vs-mean gap is a real trap.** Because expected wealth $e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}$ exceeds the median $e^{\mu}$, the "average" outcome of a volatile compounding strategy is pulled up by rare huge paths that a *typical* investor never experiences — the mathematics behind "average returns" flattering long-run reality.
