# Uniform Distribution

The uniform distribution is the picture of **"every outcome is equally likely"** — a **flat** shape with no favored region. It comes in two forms, matching the two kinds of random variable:

1. **Continuous Uniform Distribution** — a **PDF** (continuous random variable).
2. **Discrete Uniform Distribution** — a **PMF** (discrete random variable).

## 1. Continuous Uniform Distribution

**The continuous uniform distributions (or rectangular distributions) are a family of symmetric probability distributions.** Such a distribution describes an experiment where there is an arbitrary outcome that lies **between certain bounds**. The bounds are the parameters $a$ and $b$ — the **minimum** and **maximum** values.

$$
X \sim U(a, b), \qquad -\infty < a < b < \infty
$$

The density is constant on $[a,b]$ and zero elsewhere — a rectangle of height $1/(b-a)$ (chosen so the area is exactly 1):

$$
\text{PDF: } f(x) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{b-a} & x \in [a, b] \\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\qquad
\text{CDF: } F(x) =
\begin{cases}
0 & x < a \\[2pt]
\dfrac{x-a}{b-a} & x \in [a, b] \\[2pt]
1 & x > b
\end{cases}
$$

The CDF rises as a **straight line** from 0 to 1 across $[a,b]$ (using the maximum convention at the endpoints) — which is the [PMF/PDF/CDF](/docs/Probability/distributions/foundations/pmf-pdf-cdf) point made vivid: a *constant* density has a *straight-line* CDF, because the slope (the density) never changes.

$$
\text{Mean} = \tfrac{1}{2}(a+b), \qquad \text{Median} = \tfrac{1}{2}(a+b), \qquad \text{Variance} = \tfrac{1}{12}(b-a)^2
$$

### Worked example — candy sales

**The number of candies sold daily at a shop is uniformly distributed, with a maximum of 40 and a minimum of 10.** So $a = 10$, $b = 40$, and the density height is $1/(b-a) = 1/30$.

For a continuous uniform, a probability is just **width × height** — the area of a sub-rectangle:

**i) Probability daily sales fall between 15 and 30?** With $x_1 = 15,\ x_2 = 30$:

$$
P(15 \le X \le 30) = (x_2 - x_1)\times\frac{1}{b-a} = (30 - 15)\times\frac{1}{30} = \frac{15}{30} = 0.5
$$

**And $P(X > 20)$?**

$$
P(X > 20) = (40 - 20)\times\frac{1}{30} = \frac{20}{30} = 0.66 = 66\%
$$

## 2. Discrete Uniform Distribution

**The discrete uniform distribution is a symmetric probability distribution wherein a finite number of values are equally likely to be observed** — every one of $n$ values has equal probability $1/n$. Put plainly: "a known, finite number of outcomes, all equally likely to happen."

$$
X \sim U(a, b), \quad b \ge a, \qquad \text{PMF} = \frac{1}{n}, \qquad \text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \frac{a+b}{2}
$$

The canonical example is **rolling a fair die**, $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. The number of outcomes is

$$
n = b - a + 1 = 6 - 1 + 1 = 6, \qquad P(1) = P(2) = \dots = P(6) = \frac{1}{6}
$$

Six equal bars — the discrete cousin of the flat rectangle above:

<PMFExplorer kind="discrete-uniform" title="Discrete uniform — a fair die, each face 1/n" showCDF={true} />

```python
# --- continuous uniform on [a, b] ---
a, b = 10, 40
height = 1 / (b - a)                       # 1/30
p_15_30 = (30 - 15) * height               # width * height
p_gt_20 = (40 - 20) * height
print(f"P(15<=X<=30) = {p_15_30}")         # 0.5
print(f"P(X>20)      = {round(p_gt_20,2)}")# 0.67 (0.66…)
print(f"mean = {(a+b)/2},  variance = {(b-a)**2/12:.2f}")

# --- discrete uniform: a fair die ---
n = 6 - 1 + 1                              # b - a + 1
print(f"\nfair die: n={n}, each P = 1/{n} = {1/n:.4f}")
```

## Why a quant cares

The uniform is the distribution of **maximum ignorance** — no outcome preferred over another — and that makes it foundational in two ways:

- **The raw fuel of Monte Carlo.** Every simulation starts with uniform random numbers on $[0,1]$. Feed them through an **inverse CDF** and you can generate a sample from *any* distribution (inverse-transform sampling) — so pricing an exotic option by simulation ultimately rests on uniforms.
- **A "no-view" prior.** In Bayesian terms, a uniform prior says "before seeing data, I favor no value over another" — the flat starting belief that data then reshapes (see [Bayesian inference](/docs/Statistics/bayesian-inference)).
- **Discrete uniform = fair mechanisms** — a die, a lottery draw, random tie-breaking, or picking a random row for a bootstrap sample.

Its flatness is also a null hypothesis: if outcomes *should* be equally likely but aren't, the departure from uniform is the signal.
