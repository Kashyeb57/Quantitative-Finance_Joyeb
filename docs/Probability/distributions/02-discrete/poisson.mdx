# Poisson Distribution

**The Poisson distribution is a discrete probability distribution that expresses the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time** — provided those events occur with a **known constant mean rate** and **independently of the time since the last event**.

Its two defining features:

1. It's a **discrete** random variable (a **PMF**).
2. It describes the **number of events occurring in a fixed time interval**.

**Examples:** the number of people visiting a **hospital** every hour; the number of people visiting a **bank** every hour. The count is a whole number — 0, 1, 2, … — and it fluctuates around some average rate.

## The rate parameter λ

The single parameter is $\lambda$ (lambda), the **expected number of events per interval**. If a bank sees on average $\lambda = 3$ customers per hour, then across the afternoon — 12pm, 1pm, 2pm, 3pm, 4pm, 5pm — each hour brings a *random* count that averages 3 but might be 1, or 4, or 6:

$$
\lambda = 3 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{expected number of events occurring in each time interval}
$$

## The PMF

$$
P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\, \lambda^{x}}{x!}, \qquad x = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

It's defined **only at integer** $x$ (you can't have 2.5 arrivals). With $\lambda = 3$, the probability of exactly **5** customers in an hour is:

$$
P(X = 5) = \frac{e^{-3}\, 3^{5}}{5!} = \frac{0.0498 \times 243}{120} = 0.101 = 10.1\%
$$

And because it's a proper distribution you can accumulate it — e.g. $P(X \le 3) = P(0)+P(1)+P(2)+P(3)$, or combine pieces like $P(X=4) + P(X=5)$ for "4 or 5 arrivals." Drag $\lambda$ and **click a bar** (set $\lambda = 3$, click $k=5$ → $10.1\%$):

## Mean and variance — the signature property

$$
\text{Mean} = E[X] = \mu = \lambda \times t, \qquad \text{Variance} = \lambda \times t
$$

where $\lambda$ is the expected number of events per unit time and $t$ is the length of the interval. For a single unit interval this is just $\mu = \lambda$. The striking part is that the **mean and the variance are the same number** — the arrow in the notes points from mean straight to variance because they're equal:

$$
\boxed{\;\text{mean} = \text{variance} = \lambda\;}
$$

This **equidispersion** is the Poisson's fingerprint. If you measure counts and find the variance roughly equals the mean, Poisson is a good model; if the variance is much *bigger* (overdispersion), the events are clustering and you need something else.

## Poisson in 3D — how λ reshapes the counts

Let the rate $\lambda$ climb and watch the PMF over $(\lambda, k)$. At small $\lambda$ the mass sits near 0 and is lopsided; as $\lambda$ grows the hump slides right (peak near $k = \lambda$), widens, and turns **symmetric and bell-like** — for large $\lambda$ the Poisson is well approximated by a [Normal](/docs/Probability/distributions/continuous/normal).
```python
from math import exp, factorial

def poisson_pmf(x, lam):
    return exp(-lam) * lam**x / factorial(x)

# λ = 3: probability of exactly 5 events
print("P(X=5 | λ=3) =", round(poisson_pmf(5, 3), 4))          # 0.1008 ≈ 10.1%

# P(X ≤ 3)
print("P(X<=3 | λ=3) =", round(sum(poisson_pmf(k, 3) for k in range(4)), 4))

# mean = variance = λ  — confirm by simulation
import numpy as np
sample = np.random.default_rng(0).poisson(lam=3, size=100_000)
print(f"simulated mean = {sample.mean():.3f},  variance = {sample.var():.3f}  (both ≈ λ = 3)")
```

## Why a quant cares

The Poisson is the model for **rare, countable events arriving over time** — which is most of market microstructure and risk:

- **Order and trade arrivals.** The number of trades in a second, or market-orders hitting your quote, is classically Poisson — the backbone of queueing models in high-frequency trading.
- **Jumps.** In a **jump-diffusion** model of prices, the *number* of sudden jumps in an interval is Poisson; it's how you bolt crash-risk onto an otherwise smooth model.
- **Operational and credit events.** Rare losses, fraud events, or defaults in a large pool with small individual probability converge to Poisson (the "law of rare events": a Binomial with large $n$ and small $p$ becomes Poisson with $\lambda = np$).

The equidispersion assumption is the thing to test: real trade counts often **cluster** (bursts of activity), giving variance well above the mean — a signal that arrivals aren't independent and a plain Poisson understates the busy-period risk.
