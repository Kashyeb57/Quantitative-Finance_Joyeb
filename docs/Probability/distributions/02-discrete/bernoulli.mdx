# Bernoulli Distribution

**The Bernoulli distribution is the simplest discrete probability distribution.** It describes a random variable with **exactly two possible outcomes**: *success* (with probability $p$) and *failure* (with probability $1-p$). It's the model for any **binary** outcome — a coin flip, a yes/no question, up-day/down-day, default/no-default.

Two facts define it:

1. It's a **discrete** random variable (so it has a **PMF**).
2. Its outcomes are **binary** — exactly two of them.

## The outcomes, coded 0 and 1

We label the two outcomes with $K \in \{0, 1\}$:

- **Success** $\Rightarrow K = 1$, with probability $p$
- **Failure** $\Rightarrow K = 0$, with probability $q = 1 - p$

**Examples:**

- **Tossing a coin**, $\{H, T\}$: $\;P(X=H) = 0.5 = p$, and $P(X=T) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5 = q$.
- **Will a person pass or fail?** $\;P(X=\text{Pass}) = 0.4$, so $P(X=\text{Fail}) = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$.

Notice $p$ need not be $\tfrac{1}{2}$ — a Bernoulli variable can be lopsided. Three different Bernoullis:

| | $P(X=0)$ | $P(X=1)$ |
|---|---|---|
| lopsided to 1 | 0.2 | 0.8 |
| lopsided to 0 | 0.8 | 0.2 |
| fair | 0.5 | 0.5 |

### Parameters

$$
0 \le p \le 1, \qquad q = 1 - p, \qquad K \in \{0, 1\} \;\;(\text{2 outcomes})
$$

## The PMF

Take a concrete case: a company launches a **new smartphone "A"**. A person either **uses it** (probability $60\% = p$, coded $K=1$) or **doesn't** (probability $40\% = q$, coded $K=0$). That two-bar picture *is* the Bernoulli distribution. Slide $p$ and watch the bars — and the mean and variance — respond:

<PMFExplorer kind="bernoulli" title="Bernoulli PMF — one binary trial (smartphone A: p = use)" />

There's a single formula that produces both bars:

$$
\text{PMF} = p^{K} \,(1-p)^{\,1-K}
$$

It's a clever switch. Plug in each value of $K$ and one factor collapses to 1:

$$
P(K=1) = p^{1}(1-p)^{1-1} = p^{1}(1-p)^{0} = p
$$

$$
P(K=0) = p^{0}(1-p)^{1-0} = 1 \cdot (1-p)^{1} = 1 - p = q
$$

So the compact formula is just shorthand for the two-line table:

$$
\text{PMF} =
\begin{cases}
q = 1 - p & \text{if } K = 0 \\
p & \text{if } K = 1
\end{cases}
$$

## Mean, median, mode, variance

**Mean** — the expected value is the probability-weighted sum over $K \in \{0,1\}$:

$$
E[X] = \sum_{k=0}^{1} k \cdot P(k) = 0 \times 0.40 + 1 \times 0.60 = 0.60 = p
$$

The mean of a Bernoulli **is just $p$**. (With $p=0.6,\ q=0.4$: $P(1)=0.6$, $P(0)=0.4$.)

**Median** — depends on which side $p$ falls:

$$
\text{Median} =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } p < \tfrac{1}{2} \quad(\text{i.e. } q > p)\\
[0, 1] & \text{if } p = \tfrac{1}{2} \quad(q = p,\ \text{any value in } [0,1])\\
1 & \text{if } p > \tfrac{1}{2} \quad(q < p)
\end{cases}
$$

**Mode** — the more likely outcome: if $p > q$ then **$p$ (the value 1) is the mode**; otherwise $q$ (the value 0) is the mode.

**Variance** — with $P(K{=}0)=0.4=q$ and $P(K{=}1)=0.6=p$, and mean $0.6$:

$$
\sigma^2 = 0.40 \times (0 - 0.6)^2 + 0.6 \times (1 - 0.6)^2 = 0.40(0.36) + 0.6(0.16) = 0.24
$$

And $0.24 = 0.6 \times 0.4 = p \times q$. That's no coincidence — for **every** Bernoulli:

$$
\boxed{\;\sigma^2 = pq \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{pq}\;}
$$

Variance is largest at $p = 0.5$ (a fair coin is *maximally* uncertain: $\sigma^2 = 0.25$) and shrinks to 0 as $p \to 0$ or $p \to 1$ (a near-certain outcome carries almost no uncertainty). Watch $\sigma^2$ peak as you slide the lab to $p=0.5$.

```python
def bernoulli(p):
    mean = p
    var  = p * (1 - p)          # = p q
    return mean, var

for p in (0.5, 0.6, 0.9):
    m, v = bernoulli(p)
    print(f"p={p}:  mean={m:.2f}  variance=pq={v:.2f}  std={v**0.5:.3f}")

# verify the mean formula from the definition, E[X] = 0*q + 1*p
p = 0.6
print("E[X] =", 0*(1-p) + 1*p)          # -> 0.6
print("Var  =", (1-p)*(0-p)**2 + p*(1-p)**2)  # -> 0.24 = pq
```

## Why a quant cares

**The Bernoulli is the atom of every binary bet.** Strip any single-shot market event down and it's a Bernoulli trial:

- an **up day vs. down day** — $p$ is your directional edge; a no-edge coin-flip strategy is $p = 0.5$.
- a bond that **defaults or doesn't** this year — $p$ is the default probability.
- an option that expires **in- or out-of-the-money** — $p$ is $P(\text{ITM})$.
- an order that **fills or doesn't** at your limit.

The whole game is estimating $p$ and knowing your uncertainty $\sqrt{pq}$. And the moment you repeat the same trial $n$ times and count the successes, you've built the [Binomial distribution](/docs/Probability/distributions/discrete/binomial) — the next lesson, and literally "Bernoulli, $n$ times."
