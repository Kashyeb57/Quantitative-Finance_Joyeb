# Binomial Distribution

Take a [Bernoulli trial](/docs/Probability/distributions/discrete/bernoulli) and **repeat it $n$ times**, counting how many successes you get. That count is the **Binomial distribution**.

Formally: the binomial distribution with parameters $n$ and $p$ is the **discrete probability distribution of the number of successes in a sequence of $n$ independent experiments**, each asking a **yes–no question**, each with its own Boolean outcome — success (probability $p$) or failure (probability $q = 1-p$). A single success/failure experiment is a **Bernoulli trial**; a sequence of them is a **Bernoulli process**. For a single trial ($n = 1$) the binomial **is** the Bernoulli distribution. The binomial is also the basis for the **binomial test** of statistical significance.

Its three assumptions:

1. It's a **discrete** random variable (a **PMF**).
2. **Every outcome of the experiment is binary** (success/failure).
3. The experiment is **performed for $n$ trials**.

**Example:** tossing a coin **10 times**, each toss $\{H, T\}$ — here $n = 10$ and "success" = heads.

## Notation, parameters, support

$$
X \sim B(n, p)
$$

- **Parameters:** $n \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ = number of trials/experiments; $\;p \in [0, 1]$ = success probability for **each** trial; $\;q = 1 - p$.
- **Support:** $K \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots, n\}$ = the **number of successes** (you can get anywhere from 0 to all $n$).

## The PMF

$$
P(K; n, p) = \binom{n}{K}\, p^{K} (1-p)^{\,n-K}, \qquad K = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n
$$

where the **binomial coefficient** counts how many different orderings give $K$ successes:

$$
\binom{n}{K} = {}^{n}C_{K} = \frac{n!}{K!\,(n-K)!}
$$

Read the PMF as three pieces: $p^{K}$ (the $K$ successes) $\times\ (1-p)^{n-K}$ (the $n-K$ failures) $\times\ \binom{n}{K}$ (the number of ways to arrange them). Drag $n$ and $p$, and **click any bar** to read $P(X=k)$:

### Mean and variance

Because it's $n$ independent Bernoullis stacked up, the mean and variance are just $n$ times the Bernoulli's:

$$
\text{Mean} = n p, \qquad \text{Variance} = n p q, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{n p q}
$$

## Worked example 1 — exactly 3 heads in 5 flips

Coin flip: $n = 5$ trials, $p = 0.5$, and $K$ (number of heads) varies from 0 to 5. **What is the probability of getting exactly 3 heads in 5 flips?** Here $n=5$, $K=3$:

$$
P(X = 3) = \binom{5}{3}(0.5)^{3}(1 - 0.5)^{5-3} = 10 \times 0.125 \times 0.25 = 0.3125
$$

(In the lab above, set $n=5,\ p=0.5$ and click the $k=3$ bar — it reads $31.25\%$.)

## Worked example 2 — quality control

**Scenario:** inspecting **10 items** in a factory where each item has a **10% chance of being defective**. So $n = 10$ trials, $p = 0.1$ (a defective item is a "success" for counting purposes), and $K$ varies from 0 to 10. **What is the probability of finding exactly 2 defective items in a sample of 10?**

$$
P(X = 2) = \binom{10}{2}(0.1)^{2}(1 - 0.1)^{10-2} = 45 \times 0.01 \times 0.9^{8} \approx 0.1937 = 19.37\%
$$

(Set $n=10,\ p=0.1$ in the lab and click $k=2$.)

```python
from math import comb

def binom_pmf(k, n, p):
    return comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k)

# Example 1: exactly 3 heads in 5 fair flips
print("P(3 heads in 5) =", round(binom_pmf(3, 5, 0.5), 4))      # 0.3125

# Example 2: exactly 2 defectives in 10, each 10% defective
print("P(2 defective in 10) =", round(binom_pmf(2, 10, 0.1), 4))  # 0.1937

# mean and variance
n, p = 10, 0.1
print(f"mean = np = {n*p},  var = npq = {n*p*(1-p):.2f},  std = {(n*p*(1-p))**0.5:.3f}")
```

That smoothing-into-a-bell is not a coincidence — it's the [Central Limit Theorem](/docs/Probability/distributions/sampling-and-estimation/central-limit-theorem) arriving early. A binomial with large $n$ is well approximated by a [Normal](/docs/Probability/distributions/continuous/normal) with mean $np$ and variance $npq$.

## Why a quant cares

The binomial is the natural model whenever you **count successes over repeated independent bets**:

- **Win rate over a trading month.** A strategy with edge $p$ over $n$ trades: the number of winners is $B(n, p)$, with expected $np$ winners and spread $\sqrt{npq}$. That spread is why a good strategy can still have a losing month — and why you need enough $n$ to tell skill from luck.
- **Defaults in a small loan book** where each borrower independently defaults with probability $p$.
- **The binomial options-pricing model** builds a price tree from exactly this: up/down moves as repeated Bernoulli steps.

The critical caveat is the **independence** assumption. Real trades and real defaults are often *correlated* — and when they are, the true spread is far wider than $\sqrt{npq}$ suggests. (That failure of independence is the heart of the [conditional-probability](/docs/Probability/conditional-probability) lesson and the 2008 story.)
