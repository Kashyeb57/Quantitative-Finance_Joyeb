# Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem

Where the [addition rule](/docs/Probability/foundations) handles **"or,"** the **multiplication rule** handles **"and"** — the probability that two events *both* happen. And it forces the single most important question in all of probability: does one event **affect** the other?

## Independent vs. dependent events

- **Independent** — two events are independent if they **do not affect one another**. Tossing a coin, then tossing it again: the first result tells you nothing about the second — $P(H)=\tfrac{1}{2}$ on every toss, no matter what came before. Rolling a die twice is the same: $P(1)=\tfrac{1}{6}$ on the first roll and $P(2)=\tfrac{1}{6}$ on the next, each unaffected by the other.
- **Dependent** — two events are dependent if one **changes the probability** of the other. Draw a card and *keep it*, then draw again: the deck has changed, so the second probability depends on the first.

The card case makes dependence concrete. Take a King from a full deck, then a Queen from what remains:

$$
P(K) = \frac{4}{52}, \qquad P(Q \text{ after a King is removed}) = \frac{4}{51}
$$

The denominator dropped from 52 to 51 — the first draw altered the second. That's dependence.

## Conditional probability

The tool for dependence is **conditional probability** — the probability of $B$ **given that** $A$ has already happened, written $P(B \mid A)$:

$$
P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(A)}
$$

In the card example, $P(Q \mid K) = \tfrac{4}{51}$: *given* a King was drawn and set aside, all four Queens still sit in the remaining 51 cards.

## The multiplication rule

To get the probability that **both** events happen, multiply — with a twist depending on independence.

**Independent events** — just multiply the two probabilities:

$$
P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)
$$

$$
P(H \text{ and then } T) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}
$$

**Dependent events** — multiply by the *conditional* probability:

$$
P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)
$$

$$
P(K \text{ and then } Q) = \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{4}{51} = \frac{16}{2652} = \frac{4}{663} \approx 0.006
$$

Independence is simply the special case where $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$ — knowing $A$ tells you nothing, so the conditional collapses back to the plain multiplication.

```python
from fractions import Fraction

# Independent: two separate coin tosses
p_h_and_t = Fraction(1,2) * Fraction(1,2)
print(f"P(H and T), independent:  {p_h_and_t}  = {float(p_h_and_t):.3f}")

# Dependent: King then Queen from a 52-card deck, no replacement
p_king              = Fraction(4,52)
p_queen_given_king  = Fraction(4,51)          # deck now has 51 cards; all 4 queens remain
p_k_then_q          = p_king * p_queen_given_king
print(f"P(K then Q), dependent:   {p_k_then_q}  = {float(p_k_then_q):.5f}")
```

## Where this leads: Bayes' theorem

The multiplication rule can be written from either event's point of view — $P(A \text{ and } B)$ is the same thing whether you condition on $A$ or on $B$:

$$
P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)
$$

Rearrange, and out falls **Bayes' theorem** — the rule for flipping a conditional and *updating* a belief when new evidence arrives:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

That one step is the engine of statistical inference. Its full, hands-on treatment — priors, posteriors, and a live prior→posterior lab — lives in [Bayesian Inference](/docs/Statistics/bayesian-inference).

## Why a quant cares

Independence versus dependence isn't academic — it's the difference between a survivable portfolio and a blown-up one:

- **The 2008 lesson.** If mortgage defaults were **independent**, the multiplication rule makes many defaulting *at once* astronomically unlikely (multiply many small numbers → near zero). They weren't independent — they were highly **dependent**, driven by a common housing market. Models that assumed independence priced the joint risk near zero and were catastrophically wrong. Getting $P(B \mid A)$ right *is* the risk model.
- **Conditional probability is how markets actually behave.** $P(\text{big move tomorrow} \mid \text{big move today})$ is well above the unconditional $P(\text{big move})$ — that's **volatility clustering**, and it's why calm and turbulent periods come in runs.
- **Sampling without replacement** — sequential draws, position sizing from a finite pool — is the dependent-events multiplication rule in disguise.
